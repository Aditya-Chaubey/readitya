The Tree
That Asks Questions

How a stack of yes-or-no questions became one of the most useful ideas in machine learning — and why one tree is never enough.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 25 min · 9 interactives

Walk into the woods

Imagine you are standing at the edge of a wood with an empty basket. The ground is crowded with mushrooms: fat ones, spindly ones, freckled ones, a few the color of a stop sign. Some of them would make a very good dinner. Some of them would make a very bad week.

You don't know any of them. But you have a little book, and the book doesn't describe each mushroom one by one. Instead, it asks you questions. Does it grow on wood? Is there a ring around the stem? Each answer sends you to a different page, and after a few pages it tells you what you're holding.

That little book is a decision tree. So is the flowchart a nurse uses to triage patients, the script on a bank's fraud desk, and — in a much bigger, automated form — a huge share of the models that decide what loan offer you see, which transaction gets flagged, and which package gets sent on which truck.

This is a story about how a computer learns to write that little book by itself. We'll build one together, watch it get too clever, and then plant a forest.

One rule before we start: every mushroom in this story is invented, and so is the forest. Nothing here is a guide to real mushrooms. Please don't eat anything because a web page told you to.

Chapter 1

Twenty questions

A key, a basket, and the power of doubling.

Here are eight mushrooms from our imaginary wood. Below them is a key — the kind botanists have printed in field guides for more than two hundred years. Pick any mushroom, then answer the key's questions by looking at your pick.

Every time you answered, you threw away half of the possibilities. That's the whole trick. One question splits eight mushrooms into two groups of four. A second splits four into two. A third splits two into one. Three questions, eight mushrooms — because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.

Keep doubling and the numbers get silly fast. This is why the parlor game Twenty Questions is winnable at all.

Real questions are rarely perfect. "Does it grow on wood?" might split a real forest 30 to 70 instead of 50 to 50, and some answers are fuzzy. But the shape of the idea survives: a good sequence of questions narrows a huge space very quickly.

Lamarck wrote his questions by hand, from a lifetime of looking at plants. The question for the rest of this story is different: what if nobody knows the right questions? What if all you have is a pile of examples — and you want the machine to find the questions itself?

Chapter 2

A key nobody wrote

Give a computer 160 mushrooms and two rulers.

We went back to the wood and collected 160 mushrooms. For each one we measured two things: how wide the cap is and how tall the stem is. Every dot is one mushroom.

Then we sent them all to a lab. Poisonous ones are now red diamonds; edible ones are green circles. There's a pattern in there — but it's not a simple one.

A decision tree begins with one question about one measurement: is this number bigger than that threshold? The computer tries every threshold on both rulers — a few hundred candidate questions — and scores each one.

The winner: "Is the stem taller than 7.7 cm?" Above that line, most mushrooms are poisonous. Below it, most are fine. One question, and the tree is already right about 76% of the time.

Now the trick that makes it a tree: take each side and ask a new question, using only the mushrooms on that side. For short-stemmed mushrooms, the best question is about the cap: narrower than 5.6 cm? Those little buttons are trouble.

For tall-stemmed mushrooms, the cap matters too — but the threshold is different: wider than 9.9 cm? Those are big parasols, and nearly all of them are poisonous.

Repeat once more and we have three levels of questions and eight boxes. Each box is a leaf, and each leaf makes a verdict by majority vote of the mushrooms inside it. This tree gets 90% of our collection right.

Here's the rule we secretly used to invent this forest — the dashed outline. Nobody told the tree. It found the outline's rough shape from examples alone, in straight lines, one question at a time.

That's the whole idea of a learned decision tree: chop the space into boxes with straight cuts, and label each box. Two questions remain. How does it decide which cut is "best"? And when should it stop?

Chapter 3

What makes a good question?

Measuring a mess.

A good question leaves you with tidier piles than you started with. If a question splits a basket of mixed mushrooms into one basket of all-edible and one of all-poisonous, it's perfect. If both baskets end up just as mixed as before, it was a waste of breath.

To compare questions, the tree needs a number for "how mixed is this basket?" The most common choice is called Gini impurity, and it has a lovely plain-English meaning: pull out a mushroom at random, put it back, then pull out another. How often are the two different?

A pure basket scores 0: every pair matches. A perfect 50/50 basket scores 0.5, the worst you can do with two kinds. The dashed curve is entropy, a cousin from information theory that measures the same mess in bits. It's what Ross Quinlan's ID3 algorithm used in the 1980s; Gini is what CART, the other founding algorithm, used. In practice they almost always pick the same questions.

Now we can score a question. Split the basket, measure the impurity of each half, and average them — weighted by how many mushrooms landed in each. The drop from before to after is called the information gain. The best question is the one with the biggest drop.

Try being the algorithm. Drag the cut through our 160 mushrooms and watch the two baskets.

That's all "learning" means here. No intuition, no taste — just a brute-force search over every possible question, keeping the one that tidies the baskets most. With two rulers and 160 mushrooms, that's a few hundred candidates. With a thousand columns and a million rows it's a lot more, but computers are good at boring, and clever bookkeeping (sort each column once, then slide the threshold) keeps it fast.

Notice what the search can't do: it only ever looks one question ahead. It takes the best question now, and never goes back to reconsider. This is called a greedy algorithm. Finding the truly optimal tree is a much harder problem — so hard that, in general, nobody bothers. Greedy turns out to be good enough most of the time.

Chapter 4

Ask again. And again.

Recursion, leaves, and when to stop.

Once the first question is chosen, the tree does something elegantly lazy: it treats each side as a brand-new, smaller problem and runs the exact same search on it. Then again on each of those sides. Programmers call this recursion. Gardeners would call it branching.

Press play to grow the tree one level at a time. Hover any box in the diagram to see which slice of the forest it's responsible for — or hover the plot to trace a single mushroom down the tree.

Left alone, this process only stops when every leaf is perfectly pure — when every box contains mushrooms of only one kind. On our collection that takes 7 levels and 26 leaves. That sounds like a triumph. The tree is right about every single mushroom we gave it.

Look closer at the deepest boxes, though. Some are slivers holding a single mushroom. That's not a pattern. That's a tree remembering one specific mushroom.

Root

The first question, asked of every example.

Internal node

A question about one feature and one threshold.

Branch

One answer — yes or no — leading onward.

Leaf

No more questions. A verdict, by majority vote.

Depth

The longest chain of questions from root to leaf.

Chapter 5

The tree that memorized

Perfect on the past. Worse on the future.

Here's something we haven't told you. Labs make mistakes. When we invented this forest, we deliberately mislabeled about 1 in 12 mushrooms — a harmless one marked poisonous, or the other way round. Real data is like this: typos, broken sensors, judgment calls.

A tree that grows until every leaf is pure has no way to know which labels are mistakes. So it carves out a tiny box for each one — including the wrong ones. To see what that costs, we went back to the wood and picked 400 new mushrooms the tree has never seen.

Push the slider to the right. Training accuracy climbs to a perfect 100%. Accuracy on new mushrooms peaks at around depth 4 — about 85% — and then sags as the tree spends its extra questions memorizing noise.

This is overfitting, and it is the central drama of all of machine learning, not just trees. A model that fits the past perfectly has usually learned some things about the past that aren't true about the world.

A tree that is never wrong about its own examples is almost always wrong about something else.

There are a few classic ways to stop a tree before it embarrasses itself:

01

Cap the depth

Allow only so many questions in a row. Blunt, effective, easy to explain.

02

Demand big leaves

Refuse any split that would leave fewer than, say, 10 examples on a side. No slivers.

03

Prune it back

Grow the whole tree, then snip branches whose gain doesn't justify their complexity. CART's "cost-complexity pruning" does this.

04

Hold some data back

Pick the settings by checking a held-out set the tree never trained on — exactly what our 400 new mushrooms were for.

Chapter 6

A wobbly tree

Change a few mushrooms, change the whole key.

Trees have a second, quieter flaw. Because every question depends on the one before it, a tiny change near the top ripples all the way down. Swap a handful of mushrooms in the basket and the first question might change — and then every question below it changes too.

Below are three trees, each trained on a slightly different basket. Each basket was made by drawing 160 mushrooms from our collection with replacement, so some get picked twice and some not at all. Statisticians call that a bootstrap sample.

Same forest, same rulers, same algorithm — noticeably different boxes. In the jargon, a single deep tree has high variance: its answers depend a lot on exactly which examples it happened to see.

That sounds like bad news. In 2001, the statistician Leo Breiman — one of the four authors of CART — turned it into the best news trees ever got.

Chapter 7

Ask the whole forest

Many wobbly trees make one steady answer.

Breiman's idea: if every tree wobbles in a different direction, then averaging many of them should cancel the wobbles out. Grow a hundred trees, each on its own bootstrap basket. To make them even more different from each other, let each question consider only a random subset of the features. Then, for any new mushroom, let the trees vote.

He called it a random forest. Slide to add trees and watch the jagged boxes melt into something smoother.

Each tree in the forest is individually a worse model than our carefully pruned one — they're grown all the way down, memorizing their baskets. But their mistakes are scattered in different places, and a vote washes most of them out. The forest ends up beating any single tree, and it does so without anyone having to agonize over the right depth.

The same year, Jerome Friedman — another of CART's four authors — published a different way to combine trees. Instead of growing them independently and voting, gradient boosting grows them in sequence: each small tree is trained specifically to fix the errors of all the trees before it.

Boosted trees — in libraries like XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost — have dominated competitions on spreadsheet-shaped data for a decade. A 2022 study by Léo Grinsztajn and colleagues tested them against deep neural networks on 45 tabular datasets and found tree ensembles still came out ahead on typical medium-sized tables. For all the noise about giant AI models, a surprising amount of the world still runs on very large piles of very small trees.

Chapter 8

Roots and branches

Two and a half centuries of asking good questions.

  1. 1778

    Flore françoise

    Lamarck popularizes the dichotomous key: identify a plant through a chain of either-or questions.

  2. 1963

    Automatic Interaction Detection

    James Morgan and John Sonquist publish AID, an early program that splits survey data into groups automatically.

  3. 1984

    CART

    Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone's book Classification and Regression Trees sets out Gini splits and cost-complexity pruning.

  4. 1986

    ID3

    Ross Quinlan's ID3 grows trees using information gain from entropy. Its successor, C4.5, follows in 1993.

  5. 2001

    Forests and boosting

    Breiman publishes Random Forests. Friedman publishes gradient boosting machines. Trees become teams.

  6. 2016

    XGBoost

    Tianqi Chen and Carlos Guestrin's fast, scalable boosting library becomes a fixture of data-science competitions.

  7. 2022

    Still standing

    Benchmarks find tree ensembles still outperform deep learning on typical tabular data.

Why have trees lasted? Partly because they're fast and work well with messy data — mixed numbers and categories, uneven scales, missing values. But mostly, I think, because of something Lamarck understood. A small tree is something a person can read. You can print it, argue with it, and point to the exact question that made it decide. Few models in machine learning let you do that.

Where trees shine

  • Readable. A shallow tree is a flowchart you can audit.
  • Low-fuss. No need to rescale features; thresholds don't care about units.
  • Nonlinear. They capture interactions — "tall and broad" — automatically.
  • Strong in teams. Forests and boosted trees are top performers on tabular data.

Where they stumble

  • Overfitting. Left unchecked, one tree memorizes noise.
  • Wobbly. Small changes in data can rewrite the whole tree.
  • Staircase edges. Every cut is parallel to an axis, so diagonal boundaries become steps.
  • Lost readability. A forest of 500 deep trees is accurate — and no longer a flowchart.

Go back to the edge of the wood. You have a basket and a little book of questions. Now you know where the book could come from: a few hundred labeled mushrooms, a way to measure a mess, and the patience to try every question and keep the best one. You know it will be tempted to memorize, and you know to stop it early. And if you're unsure, you know to ask a hundred slightly different books and go with the majority.

That's not a bad way to make decisions, as it happens. Even for people.

Methods & notes

How the data and models were made

The mushrooms, the forest and the field-key species are all invented. Each mushroom has two measurements drawn uniformly at random (cap width 2–16 cm, stem height 1–13 cm). A hidden rule marks three regions as poisonous; then 8% of labels are flipped at random to simulate lab errors. The training set has 160 mushrooms and the "new mushrooms" test set has 400, drawn the same way with a different random seed.

Every tree on this page is computed live in your browser by a small CART-style implementation: binary splits at midpoints between observed values, Gini impurity, greedy selection, majority-vote leaves. The depth slider in Chapter 5 shows the fully grown tree truncated at each depth. The random forest uses 100 trees, each grown to purity on a bootstrap sample; because there are only two features, each split considers one randomly chosen feature (in practice the default is often the square root of the number of features).

All figures are drawn with canvas and SVG; no external charting libraries are used. Numbers in the text are filled in from the same computations.
