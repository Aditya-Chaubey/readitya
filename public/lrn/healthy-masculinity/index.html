lrn · No. 12 · Character

A Good Man.

What it takes to be a man people can trust in the modern world. Strong and gentle, steady and warm, useful and kind, and someone the people around you can count on when it matters.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 45 min · 8 interactives

Begin

At dinner, a friend makes himself the centre of the table by taking a shot at the quietest person there. Everyone laughs, a little too loudly. Later, at home, you realise you left your partner to organise the whole evening while you got to be the charming one.

Both moments ask the same question: what do you do when being decent costs you something?

For most of history, the answer to “what makes a good man?” came prepackaged. Be strong. Provide. Don't cry. Don't need anyone. Win. Some of that script was wise, some of it was cruel, and a lot of it simply doesn't fit the world most men now live in: one where partners are equals, where work is mostly done with minds rather than muscles, where loneliness is a quiet epidemic and where the biggest threats to men are often the ones inside their own heads.

This guide doesn't throw out the old ideals. It points them somewhere better.

Courage, strength, discipline, loyalty and competence are still the backbone of a good man. The question is whom they serve. Strength that protects the vulnerable is admirable; strength that intimidates them isn't. Self-reliance that lets you carry your share is a virtue; self-reliance that won't let you ask for help when you're drowning is a trap. The qualities here aren't exclusively male, and nothing in this guide says women can't have them. But for men who grew up hearing that fear, tenderness or asking for help made them less of a man, making room for those things can feel deeply personal.

We'll draw on a long tradition, from Aristotle and the Stoics to The Art of Manliness, from the 80-year Harvard Study of Adult Development to marriage research and modern psychology, and on men worth learning from, in history and in fiction. And we'll keep it practical, with scripts, exercises and a four-week plan.

✦

01

Character

What do people get to count on?

Reputation is what people say about you. Character is what they can predict about you.

The Art of Manliness republishes an 1881 essay by the Scottish writer Samuel Smiles that makes a useful distinction between social standing and character. Character, he argued, is the trust other people place in the way you live, and it's worth more than wealth, cleverness or status. A modern version of that idea widens the circle of people whose judgment matters: the colleague you supervise, the woman who disagrees with you, the man whose life looks different from yours, the waiter, and the people who see you at home after your patience has run out. [1]

Here are eight qualities that turn up, in one form or another, in almost every serious attempt to describe a good man, from ancient virtue ethics to modern psychology. Tap each to see what it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday.

These qualities work together, and each one goes bad without the others. Courage needs judgment about which risks are worth taking. Loyalty needs enough honesty to challenge a friend who's hurting someone. Generosity needs boundaries so the help you give can be sustained. Discipline without warmth becomes rigidity; warmth without discipline becomes unreliability. If your best trait keeps giving you a reason to ignore the effects of your behaviour, someone close to you can probably explain what's missing.

02

The golden mean

Too little, too much, just right

Aristotle's 2,300-year-old insight: most virtues sit between two vices.

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle argued that a virtue is usually a balance point between two failures, one of too little and one of too much. Courage sits between cowardice and recklessness. Generosity sits between stinginess and wastefulness. Proper pride sits between self-abasement and vanity.

That idea is almost tailor-made for modern masculinity, because most of the damage done by “toxic” versions of manhood comes from good traits pushed too far, and much of the damage done by “soft” versions comes from good traits not pushed far enough. Drag the needle for each trait and see where the line falls.

Aristotle added a subtle point: the mean isn't the same for everyone or every situation. A soldier needs more boldness than an accountant; a man recovering from burnout needs more rest than one who is coasting. The right amount is the one a wise person would choose here, now, for these people. That's why character isn't a rulebook. It's judgment, practised until it becomes habit.

03

The old codes, rewritten

Protect, provide, pass it on

The ancient job description for men, updated for a world of equals.

In his 1990 book Manhood in the Making, the anthropologist David Gilmore studied cultures around the world and found a surprisingly common pattern: manhood was something earned, not given, and it usually centred on three duties, often summarised as protect, provide and procreate. The Art of Manliness has written a long series on these “3 P's”, arguing they still capture something real about what men are drawn to. [2]

The impulse behind them, to be useful, to stand between the people you love and harm, to build something that outlasts you, is worth keeping. What's changed is what each one looks like. Flip the cards.

04

Strength and competence

Become hard to break and easy to rely on

Capability is a form of kindness. The more you can do, the more you can do for others.

Strength still matters. Not the kind that wins bar fights, but the kind that lets you carry your father's suitcase up four flights of stairs, stay calm when the car breaks down on a highway at night, and still have energy for your kids at 8 p.m. A good man looks after his body because other people depend on it.

The same goes for competence. Know how to feed yourself and others. Learn to manage your obligations, keep track of your money, and take decent care of the place you live. Practise a craft for the satisfaction of getting better. Treat rest and routine care as part of maintaining your capacity, not as weakness. And spend some of what you learn on other people: teach a new teammate, take a regular turn helping a neighbour, or mentor someone without expecting to shape their life in your image.

05

Composure and emotional courage

Stay present when your pride gets loud

Feeling everything, and choosing what you do with it.

The old script said a strong man feels nothing. The truth is that a strong man feels everything and still chooses well. You can be angry and speak with care. You can be scared and make a decision. Composure is the interval between what you feel and what you choose to do. Marcus Aurelius, a Roman emperor who spent much of his reign at war, wrote reminders to himself to stay steady under provocation; nearly two thousand years later, his private notebook, the Meditations, is still read by soldiers, athletes and executives.

You build that interval by noticing the impulse early and giving yourself enough time to choose a response you can stand behind. Try a short sequence:

1

Notice

Catch the cue in your body: a tight jaw, heat in your face, the urge to fire off a long text.

2

Name

Name what might be underneath: embarrassment, worry, hurt, fear or anger. Naming a feeling tends to turn its volume down.

3

Choose

Pick one next action. Ask a question. Make a direct request. If you need a pause, say when you'll come back, and come back then.

A pause that stretches into days of silence leaves the other person carrying the conflict. Composure isn't withdrawal.

What the research found

A 2017 meta-analysis of 78 samples found that stronger conformity to certain masculine norms was linked to worse mental-health outcomes and less willingness to seek help. The associations varied by norm, with rigid self-reliance, playboy attitudes and power over women among the most consistently harmful. Other norms, like putting work first, showed weaker links. The lesson isn't that masculinity is bad, but that some of its rules are expensive. [3]

Ask for backup

Share what you're carrying with someone who has room to hear it. “I've been having a rough week. Do you have fifteen minutes to talk?” gives a friend a clear way to show up. When distress keeps disrupting your days, bring in a qualified professional. Knowing when to ask for backup is part of looking after yourself, in the same way a good soldier calls for support instead of dying alone to prove a point. In India, the government's free Tele-MANAS mental health helpline is 14416, available around the clock in many languages.

Get good at repair

Every man will lose his temper, say the wrong thing or let someone down. What separates good men is what happens next. When you cross a line, name the action and its effect, say what you'll do differently, and give the other person time. A real apology has no “but” in it.

Repair continues when the next meeting arrives. A pattern of apologies with the same behaviour teaches people what to expect.

What could you say?

Some of the hardest moments of being a decent man last about ten seconds. Pick a situation, think about what you'd say, then reveal a version that works.

06

Having class

The courtesy you extend when nobody important is watching

Pay attention to the person with the least leverage in the room.

“Class” can refer to wealth and social position. Here it means a kind of consideration. A well-cut jacket can help you dress for an occasion. Your treatment of the people working that occasion says more about how you carry yourself. There's an old saying, often repeated in business: you can learn everything about a person from how they treat the waiter.

Make the room easier to be in

Learn names. Introduce the person who arrived alone, and give the two people a subject they can actually talk about. Put away your phone when someone tells you something that matters. In a meeting, if someone gets interrupted, say, “I'd like to hear how you were going to finish that.” At dinner, a joke everyone can enjoy gives you more room than a joke someone has to pretend to enjoy.

Show up with care

Clean clothes that fit, tidy shoes, and a little attention to the setting go a long way. Different rooms have different customs; ask the host what's expected if you're unsure. Be punctual where people depend on you, send word when you're delayed, and thank the people who did the work. If something goes wrong with an order, be specific and courteous with the person who can fix it.

Be discreet and direct

Someone else's confidence stays with you. Give useful feedback in private where possible, and credit people by name in public. You can decline an invitation warmly and clearly. You can also call out disrespect without turning the correction into a performance: start with the cleanest sentence that addresses the behaviour, then give the other person room to respond.

07

Partnership and respect

Be someone an equal can build a life with

Confidence, consent, and carrying your share of the invisible work.

Confidence makes it possible to state an interest clearly and leave someone free to respond. You can ask someone out, check what they want, and accept disappointment graciously. In intimacy, check that what you're doing is mutually wanted. Slow down when someone seems unsure. Anyone can change their mind. Respect extends to texts, photos, passwords and what your partner shares in confidence. [5]

Accept influence

The psychologist John Gottman, who has studied thousands of couples, found that one of the strongest predictors of a stable, happy marriage was whether the husband was willing to accept influence from his wife: to take her opinions seriously, share decision-making and sometimes change course because of what she said. Men who refused to share power were far more likely to see their marriages fail. Strength, it turns out, includes being moved. [6]

Take ownership of the whole task

India's 2024 Time Use Survey found that women who do unpaid domestic work spend about 289 minutes a day on it, compared with about 88 minutes for men who do any; and only about a quarter of men did any at all. [7] Much of the gap is invisible: remembering, planning, noticing, deciding. If you take on dinners, that means knowing what's in the fridge, checking schedules, getting groceries, cooking and seeing the kitchen through afterwards, not waiting to be told. Ask which parts of your shared life currently depend on one person keeping everything in their head. Decide together what each person can fully own, from noticing to finishing, and revisit the agreement when circumstances change.

Let other people keep their agency

Offer help as a choice. Ask whether someone wants company, practical help or space. In a disagreement, you can set a limit on what you're willing to do and respect the other person's right to decide for themselves. At work, give a colleague real authority over a project and accept that they may take a different route. At home, let your partner handle a conversation they asked to handle themselves. Equimundo's research puts caring relationships and cooperation near the centre of a healthier vision of manhood. Most care looks ordinary while it's happening. Its value becomes obvious when nobody does it. [8]

08

Friendship

Build a life with other people in it

The friendship recession, and how to be the man who ends it.

The longest-running study of adult life ever conducted, the Harvard Study of Adult Development, has followed hundreds of men since 1938 (and later their children). Its directors, Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz, sum up its biggest finding plainly: the quality of our relationships is the strongest predictor of how healthy and happy we are as we age, more than wealth, fame or social class. [9]

And yet many men are losing their friends. In a 2021 US survey, the share of men saying they had no close friends at all had risen to 15 percent, up from 3 percent in a 1990 poll, and the share with six or more close friends had halved. (The two surveys used different methods, so the exact jump may be overstated, but the direction is clear.) [10]

The Art of Manliness suggests using shared activities to make and keep friendships: a running group, a recurring dinner, a volunteer shift or a weekly call gives people a reason to be in the same place again and again. Over time, make room for a little more honesty. Ask a specific question about something your friend mentioned last week. Tell him a little about what actually happened in yours. [11]

Movember's research on the “perception gap” suggests men often underestimate how open other men are to friendship and to talking about their struggles. So make the invitation yourself. Put a day and a time on it. If a friend tells you he's having a hard time, listen for what he needs, and check in again a few days later. Give your closest friendships some of the calendar space you already reserve for work. [12]

09

Fathers, mentors and models

Be the man someone younger is watching

Boys learn what a man is mostly by watching one.

The psychologist Erik Erikson called the great task of mid-life generativity: caring for the next generation, whether through children, students, apprentices or a community. You don't need to be a father to do it, and you don't need to wait for mid-life. A younger cousin, a junior colleague, the kid in your building who plays cricket badly: someone is learning from how you behave, whether you mean to teach them or not.

If you are a father, your presence matters more than your paycheque. Decades of research link involved, warm fathering with better outcomes for children's emotional and academic development. That means doing the school run, knowing your child's friends' names, saying “I love you” and “I'm proud of you” out loud, and letting them see you apologise when you're wrong. Your son learns how to treat women by watching how you treat his mother. Your daughter learns what to expect from men the same way.

11

An everyday code

Twelve lines to live by

The whole guide, short enough to remember.

  1. Keep your word, or say early and honestly when you can't.
  2. Use your strength to protect, never to intimidate.
  3. Treat the person with the least power in the room best.
  4. Feel everything. Choose what you do with it.
  5. Apologise without a “but”, then change the behaviour.
  6. Carry your share of the work, including the invisible part.
  7. Take “no” gracefully, every time, from everyone.
  8. Ask for help before you need rescuing.
  9. Call your friends. Put a date on it.
  10. Become useful at something, and teach it.
  11. Speak up when someone is being cruel.
  12. Live so that someone younger is better for watching you.

12

Practice

Give it four weeks

Character isn't something you have. It's something you do, repeatedly.

Pick a day for each task. At the end of the week, write down what you did and what the other person experienced. If you miss a week, shrink the task until it fits your life, then begin again.

At the end of the month, ask someone who knows you well: “Where have I been easier to trust, and where do I still make things harder than they need to be?” Give them time to answer. Don't defend yourself. The next good thing to practise is probably in what they tell you.

Being a good man isn't a title you earn once. It's a thousand small choices, most of them made when nobody's keeping score: the joke you didn't laugh at, the call you made, the dishes you did without being asked, the apology you gave first. Make enough of them, and one day you'll notice people trust you. That's the whole thing.
