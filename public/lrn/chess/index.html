The Long Game

Chess for adults

The opening move

It is not too late. It is the perfect time.

Most people learn chess at nine, forget it at eleven, and spend the next twenty years vaguely remembering that the horse moves in an L. Then something happens. A friend sets up a board at a party, or a series about an orphan prodigy turns up on Netflix, or they watch an eighteen-year-old from Chennai become world champion. They think: I wish I were good at this.

This guide is for that person. It is for the thirty-year-old who plays on the metro, the forty-year-old who wants a hobby that isn’t a screen pretending to be a hobby, and anyone who has ever lost a game in eleven moves and wondered what on earth happened. It doesn’t assume you remember the rules. It does assume you are an adult, which means you want to know why, you have limited time, and you can tell the difference between a shortcut and a good habit.

Here is the good news, backed by research we will get to: adults learn chess well. You won’t become a grandmaster, and neither will almost anyone who starts at any age. But beating most of the people you know, understanding what masters are doing, and reaching a solid club rating are open to anyone willing to practise a little, the right way. And the game rewards you long before you are good at it.

    IThe board & the army

    Sixty-four squares, thirty-two pieces, one goal

    Chess is a war game with perfect information. Nothing is hidden and nothing is left to chance: no dice, no cards. Both armies are identical, and White moves first. You win by attacking the enemy king so that it cannot escape. That is checkmate, from the Persian shāh māt: “the king is helpless.”

    The game was born in India around the sixth century as chaturanga, named after the four divisions of an ancient army: infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots. Those became our pawns, knights, bishops and rooks. It travelled to Persia as shatranj, spread with the Islamic world, and reached Europe around the year 1000. The pieces were slow back then. The game we play today was born in Spain around 1475, when the queen, until then a feeble piece that crept one square diagonally, suddenly gained her modern power. Contemporaries called the new version “mad queen’s chess”. Games became faster, sharper and more beautiful, and the rules have barely changed since.

    The six pieces

    Each side starts with eight pawns, two rooks, two knights, two bishops, a queen and a king. The board is placed so that each player has a light square in the bottom-right corner (“light on the right”), and the queen starts on her own colour: white queen on a light square, black queen on a dark one. Then pick up a piece below and see what it can do.

    The rules everyone forgets

    Castling

    Once per game, the king moves two squares towards a rook, and the rook hops over to the other side. It is the only move where two pieces move at once. You can’t castle if the king or that rook has already moved, if a piece stands between them, if you are in check, or if the king would pass through or land on an attacked square.

    En passant

    If a pawn makes its two-square first move and lands right beside an enemy pawn, that enemy pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square. The catch: it must be done immediately, on the very next move, or the right is lost. French for “in passing”.

    Promotion

    A pawn that reaches the far rank becomes a queen, rook, bishop or knight, your choice, even if you still have your original queen. Two queens is legal, and sometimes necessary.

    Check, mate & stalemate

    A king under attack is “in check” and you must get out of it at once: move, block or capture. No way out is checkmate, and the game is over. If the side to move is not in check but has no legal move, it is stalemate, a draw. Stalemate rescues more losing positions than any other trick.

    Other draws

    A game is drawn by agreement; when the same position appears three times; after fifty moves by each side with no capture or pawn move; or when neither side has enough material left to mate (a lone king plus a single bishop or knight cannot force it).

    Notation

    Squares are named by file (a–h, left to right from White’s side) and rank (1–8). Moves are written with a piece letter (K, Q, R, B, N; pawns get none) and the square: Nf3, e4. “x” is a capture, “+” check, “#” mate, and O-O / O-O-O are short and long castling.

    That last point matters more than it looks. Reading notation unlocks every chess book ever written, and adults who learn it early improve faster because they can study. The quickest way in is to learn the board itself.

    IIThe science of getting better

    Masters don’t think faster. They see differently.

    In the 1940s, the Dutch psychologist and chess master Adriaan de Groot asked strong players and world-class grandmasters to think aloud while choosing moves. He expected the best players to calculate deeper or consider more options. They didn’t. Grandmasters looked at about as many moves as weaker players. The difference was that the right moves came to them first.

    Then he showed players a real position for a few seconds and asked them to reconstruct it. Masters rebuilt it almost perfectly. Club players managed a handful of pieces. In 1973 William Chase and Herbert Simon repeated the experiment with a twist: they also used random positions, pieces scattered without any chess logic. The master’s magic vanished. On random boards, everyone remembered just a few pieces.

    The explanation, called chunking, is the most useful idea in this guide. A master doesn’t see twenty-five pieces. They see six or seven familiar patterns: a castled king, a fianchettoed bishop, a pawn chain. Each pattern is one “chunk” in memory, with good moves attached. Simon and his colleagues estimated that a master has tens of thousands of these patterns. Later work by Fernand Gobet suggested masters do keep a small edge even on random boards, but the lesson stands: chess skill is mostly stored knowledge of patterns, built by exposure to real positions. Try it yourself.

    So how do you build the patterns?

    Through practice, but the kind matters. In a 2005 study, Neil Charness and colleagues surveyed hundreds of tournament players about how they had spent their chess lives. The strongest predictor of skill was serious study alone: solving positions, analysing games, working through books. Not tournament play, and certainly not casual blitz. The grandmasters in their samples had put in roughly 5,000 hours of solitary study in their first decade of serious play, about five times as much as intermediate players.

    That doesn’t mean hours are everything. When Guillermo Campitelli and Fernand Gobet surveyed Argentine players, they found that every master had practised at least about 3,000 hours, but some players with far more practice never got there. A later reanalysis by David Hambrick and colleagues found that among grandmasters, the practice they had needed ranged from about 800 to over 24,000 hours. People differ, a lot. Practice is necessary, not sufficient, and that is liberating. You are not racing anyone. You are getting better than you were.

    Solve, don’t scroll

    Puzzles are concentrated pattern training. Fifteen focused minutes beat an hour of half-watching videos.

    Play slow, then look back

    Play games long enough to think (15 minutes or more each), then review them. The review is where you learn.

    Replay master games

    Cover the moves and guess each one. It fills your head with good patterns, quickly.

    “Aren’t I too old?”

    Starting age matters for the very top. Gobet and Campitelli found that players who began serious chess before thirteen were far more likely to reach international level, and almost every elite grandmaster started young. But that is a question about the last one per cent. For everything below it, adults have real advantages: patience, the habit of studying, money for a good book, and the ability to sit with a problem.

    Ageing is kinder to chess players than you might fear. Roring and Charness, studying thousands of FIDE-rated players across the lifespan, found that decline after the peak is gradual, gentler for players who start strong, and that staying active helps. Experienced older players also find good moves more efficiently, searching less but recognising more. Chess is one of the few competitive pursuits you can keep improving at in your forties and still enjoy in your eighties.

    When you see a good move, look for a better one.

    Chess proverb, often credited to Emanuel Lasker

    There is a warning built into all this pattern power. In 2008 Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod and Fernand Gobet showed experts positions with a familiar, decent solution and a shorter, better one. The experts said they were looking for alternatives, but eye-tracking showed their gaze kept returning to the familiar idea. Psychologists call it the Einstellung effect: the first good idea blinds you to a better one. The fix is a habit we will build in Chapter VIII: always ask what else there is.

    IIIThe first dozen moves

    Openings are not memory tests. They are a race to get dressed.

    The opening is the first ten to fifteen moves, when both sides bring their pieces into play. There are thousands of named lines, millions of words of theory, and an entire industry selling courses. Ignore nearly all of it for now. At club level, games are decided by tactics and blunders long before opening theory matters. What you need is a handful of principles and one or two simple setups you understand.

    1. Fight for the centre. The four squares d4, e4, d5 and e5 are the high ground. Pieces in the centre reach more squares. Start with a centre pawn: 1.e4 or 1.d4.
    2. Develop your pieces. Bring knights and bishops off the back rank, usually towards the centre. Each move should bring a new piece into play.
    3. Castle early. Usually within the first ten moves. It tucks the king away and connects the rooks.
    4. Don’t move the same piece twice without a good reason, and don’t grab pawns at the cost of development.
    5. Keep the queen at home for a while. Bring her out too early and enemy pieces develop by chasing her.
    6. Connect your rooks and put them on open or half-open files. When the back rank is clear between them, the opening is done.

    Watch those principles at work in some of the most respected openings ever played, plus two traps every beginner should know. The tracker counts each side’s progress: centre pawns, minor pieces developed, and whether the king is safe.

    Italian Game

      An adult’s repertoire

      Pick one opening as White and one defence each against 1.e4 and 1.d4, and stick with them for months. You learn far more by seeing the same structures again and again than by dabbling. Good choices for improving adults:

      As White

      The Italian Game (classical, teaches every principle) or the London System (the same setup against almost anything; lovely when you are short on study time).

      Against 1.e4

      1…e5, leading to open, principled games; or the Caro-Kann, solid and hard to blow up.

      Against 1.d4

      The Queen’s Gambit Declined, a defence world champions have trusted for over a century.

      Learn the first six to eight moves and, more importantly, the ideas: where your pieces belong, which pawn breaks you want, and what the typical plans are. When your opponent leaves the book on move four, which they will, the principles take over.

      IVWhere games are won

      “Chess is ninety-nine per cent tactics”

      The line is usually credited to the German master Richard Teichmann, and it is an exaggeration that is nearly true below master level. A tactic is a short, forcing sequence (checks, captures, threats) that wins material or delivers mate. Strategy tells you where to go. Tactics are how you actually get there, and how you lose when you are not careful.

      Almost every tactic is built from a few basic motifs. Learn their names, because naming a pattern helps you spot it. Tap each card.

      Loose pieces drop off

      Grandmaster John Nunn boiled amateur chess down to four letters: LPDO, “loose pieces drop off”. A loose piece is one that is undefended, or attacked by something cheaper than itself. Most tactics, forks especially, feed on loose pieces. Training your eye to spot them, on both sides of the board, is the fastest single upgrade you can make.

      How to train tactics

      Solve puzzles every day, a few at a time, and prize accuracy over speed. Calculate the whole line before you move, as you would in a game, rather than trying moves to see if the app approves. When you get one wrong, set it up and work out why. Repetition is powerful: the Woodpecker Method of grandmasters Axel Smith and Hans Tikkanen has you solve the same set of puzzles several times, faster each cycle, until the patterns become reflexes. Both Lichess and Chess.com have huge free puzzle libraries sorted by theme.

      VThe patterns of the end

      Checkmates have shapes

      Mate rarely appears from nowhere. Again and again the same few geometries show up: a king boxed in by its own pawns, a knight jumping into a crowded corner, two bishops crossing like scissors. Players have named them for centuries. Once you know them, you start seeing them in your games two or three moves early, for your opponent as well as for you.

      VIThinking in plans

      When there is no tactic, improve something

      Most positions don’t contain a combination. What do you do then? You make the position better, one move at a time. Strategy is the art of knowing what “better” means: which pieces are good, which squares matter, which pawns are strong and which are a liability.

      Wilhelm Steinitz, the first world champion, was the first to describe chess this way. Small, lasting advantages add up, and the side that collects more of them eventually gets a winning tactic as a reward. Here are the ideas that matter most.

      Three questions for any quiet position

      1. Which is my worst piece? Find the piece doing the least, a bishop staring at its own pawns or a knight on the rim, and give it a job. It is the most reliable plan in chess.
      2. Where are the weaknesses? Look for loose pawns, holes your opponent can no longer guard with a pawn, and a king without cover. Aim your pieces at them.
      3. What does my opponent want? Before improving your own position, ask what they would do if it were their move again. Stopping their plan in advance is called prophylaxis, and it is a hallmark of strong players.

      VIIWhere the king fights

      Study the end at the beginning

      José Raúl Capablanca, the Cuban world champion famous for making chess look effortless, opened his classic Chess Fundamentals with endings, not openings. With few pieces on the board, you see clearly what each piece can do. The king, a coward all game, becomes a fighter. And because so many games come down to a few pieces, knowing a handful of endings wins you points that your opponents throw away.

      Start with pawn endings. The first idea is a lovely shortcut called the rule of the square. Can a king catch a runaway pawn? Draw a square from the pawn to its promotion rank. If the defending king is inside that square, or can step into it on its move, it catches the pawn. If not, the pawn queens. No counting needed.

      Study 08 · The rule of the square

      Can the king catch the pawn?

      Tap any empty square to place the black king. The gold area is the pawn’s “square”, running from the pawn to its queening rank. With White to move, the king must already stand inside it. With Black to move, it is enough that the king can step into it.

      A pawn on its starting square can move two squares at once, so count it as if it stood one square further up.

      Opposition: when kings stand face to face

      When two kings stand facing each other with one square between them, the side that does not have to move is said to “have the opposition”. Neither king may step next to the other, so the side to move must give way. In king-and-pawn endings, this tiny duel decides whether a pawn promotes or the game is drawn. The rule of thumb: in front of your pawn, take the opposition; behind it, your king should lead the pawn, not follow it.

      The first mate every adult should own

      King and queen against a lone king is a certain win, but beginners botch it all the time, usually by stalemating. The method is to use the queen to build a box around the enemy king, shrink it a knight’s move away, never giving pointless checks, and then march your own king up to help. The queen can’t mate alone. Practise it here until you can do it in under twenty moves.

      VIIIA routine for every move

      Most games are lost, not won

      At club level, the player who blunders less usually wins. And most blunders are not deep mistakes. They are skipped steps: a move played without noticing that the opponent’s last move created a threat, or that the piece you just moved was guarding something. The cure is a short routine, used on every move of every slow game, until it becomes automatic.

      1

      What did they just do?

      Every move changes something. What does their last move attack? What did it stop defending? Is there a threat?

      2

      Checks, captures, threats

      Scan every forcing move, for you and for them. Forcing moves are where tactics live.

      3

      Candidate moves

      Alexander Kotov’s advice: list two or three sensible options before you analyse any of them. This is the antidote to Einstellung.

      4

      Calculate & compare

      Look at the forcing lines first. Picture the position after each candidate and ask which leaves you better.

      5

      Blunder check

      Before you touch the piece: what are their checks, captures and threats after my move? Is anything left loose?

      It sounds slow, and at first it is. That is why improving players should play mostly slow games: rapid at fifteen minutes or more per side, or classical. With practice the routine compresses into a few seconds of habit, the way checking your mirrors does when you drive.

      Time, tilt and the mind

      Use your clock. Beginners often lose with half their time unused, having played critical moves instantly. Spend time at the moments that matter: when the position changes character, after captures, and when you are about to commit to a plan. Conversely, don’t sink twenty minutes into the opening and then blitz out the middlegame.

      Then there is tilt, the poker word for playing badly because you are upset. After a painful loss, the urge to immediately play again (“just one more”) is strong and almost always a mistake. Losing streaks online usually come from playing angry or tired. A good rule: after two losses in a row, stop and do something else. Review the games tomorrow.

      IXClocks, ratings & real opponents

      Where, how fast, and against whom

      You can play chess against anyone in the world at any hour, for free. The two big sites are Lichess, free, open-source and ad-free, run as a charity; and Chess.com, the largest, with lessons, bots and a polished app. Both are excellent. Play on whichever you enjoy, and use both for puzzles and analysis.

      BulletUnder 3 min

      Reflexes and mouse speed. Fun, addictive, terrible for learning.

      Blitz3–10 min

      Fast pattern recognition. Good as a treat, not a diet.

      Rapid10–60 min

      Enough time to think. The sweet spot for improvers: try 15+10.

      Classical60 min +

      Tournament chess. Deep, tiring, and the most instructive of all.

      Notation like “15+10” means fifteen minutes per player, plus ten seconds added after every move (the increment). Increments are kind to adults. You will never lose a won position just because the clock ran out.

      What your rating means

      Every rated player has a number, based on the system the Hungarian-American physics professor Arpad Elo designed and FIDE adopted in 1970. Your rating rises when you score better than expected and falls when you score worse, and the expectation depends on the gap between you and your opponent. A 200-point gap means the stronger player is expected to score about 76 per cent. A 400-point gap, about 91 per cent. Online ratings run on similar systems (Lichess uses Glicko-2) but on different scales, so don’t compare your Chess.com number with a FIDE one.

      Over the board: the etiquette

      Playing a person across a real board is a different joy: the weight of the pieces, the click of the clock, the handshake. A few customs, some of them actual rules, will make you welcome anywhere.

      Touch-move

      In serious games, if you deliberately touch one of your pieces, you must move it if it has a legal move. Touch an enemy piece and you must capture it if you can. Let go of a piece on a square and the move stands.

      “J’adoube”

      To straighten a crooked piece, say “adjust” or “j’adoube” (French: “I adjust”) before touching it. Only on your own turn.

      Shake hands

      Before and after the game. Win or lose, a handshake and “good game” are the custom everywhere.

      Resign with grace

      When the position is hopeless, tip your king over or offer your hand. No sighing, no excuses. Among beginners, though, play on: your opponent may yet stalemate you.

      Quiet hands

      No talking during play, no tapping, no hovering your hand over the board. Press the clock with the same hand that moved the piece.

      Phones away

      At tournaments, having a phone on you can mean an instant loss. Engines in pockets are the scourge of modern chess, and arbiters are strict.

      To find over-the-board chess, look for a local club (many cities have weekend meetups in cafés and parks), or enter a rated tournament. In India, tournaments are organised under the All India Chess Federation, and rated events run almost every weekend in major cities. Your first tournament will be nerve-racking and wonderful. Bring water, a pen for your scoresheet and a snack, and expect to lose a couple of games. Everybody does.

        The last word

        A game of chess is a conversation between two minds, conducted entirely in moves. You will lose hundreds of games on the way to getting good, and that is not failure. It is how the patterns get in. Every strong player you admire has lost more games than you have played.

        So set up a board. Play slowly. Look at what your opponent just did. Check for loose pieces. Castle. And when you see a good move, look for a better one. The game has waited fifteen hundred years for you. It is happy to wait while you think.
