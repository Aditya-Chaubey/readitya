01 / THE IDEA

What investing actually does

Investing means putting money into an asset with the expectation of future income, growth in value, or both. In exchange for that possibility, you give up some certainty about what the money will be worth when you need it.

Suppose you have ₹10,000. In a savings account, its job might be to cover a repair tomorrow. In a stock fund, its job might be to help pay for life twenty years from now. The balances start at the same number, but the money has different jobs. The second can fall sharply just when you need it. The first can steadily lose buying power if interest lags inflation.

Behind every investment, ask where the return comes from. A share represents part of a company that may generate more profit over time. A bond is a loan whose borrower promises interest and repayment. A rented property can pay rent and change in price. Gold pays no income, so your return depends on the price someone else will pay. This one question helps you tell a credible investment thesis from a sales pitch.

Saving

Keep money accessible for bills, emergencies and known short-term needs. The terms of the account or deposit matter.

Investing

Take measured risk to pursue a future goal. Expected return is an estimate. Actual return is what happened.

Trading

Seek gains from changes in price, often over shorter periods. Execution, costs and timing play a bigger role.

An investor’s edge can be very ordinary. You can wait, spread risk, keep costs down and save on a schedule. Your returns remain uncertain. These choices improve the parts of the outcome you can control. SEC investing basics

02 / BEFORE YOU BUY

Give every rupee a job

A portfolio starts with a deadline and a cash flow. Choose investments after you know how much you can put away and when you might have to take it back.

Protect the money you can’t afford to lose

  1. Find your investable surplus. Start with take-home income. Set aside essential expenses, minimum debt payments, necessary insurance, irregular bills and the cash you need to keep available. Build a recurring contribution around what remains in an ordinary month.
  2. Build an emergency buffer. Three to six months of essential spending is a common starting heuristic. Irregular income, dependents or a difficult job market can justify more. Keep this money easy to reach and learn the withdrawal and deposit-protection rules.
  3. Deal with expensive debt. Paying down a balance charging 24% can avoid a high, known interest cost. A steady 24% investment return is a very different proposition. Keep a buffer and check early-repayment terms before committing extra cash.
  4. Separate your goals. Write down the amount, target date, currency and flexibility for each. A ₹2 lakh course fee due in eighteen months needs different treatment from retirement spending that can be adjusted over decades.
Put dates on the money

For a fixed, near-term bill, protecting principal and access generally matter most. For a flexible goal several years away, mix growth and stability according to what a delay would cost you. For a distant goal, consider growth assets if your finances and temperament can absorb sustained declines. Move money needed soon into suitable lower-risk assets as the date approaches.

How much do you actually need?

Start in today’s money. A ₹10 lakh goal ten years away would cost roughly ₹16.29 lakh if its price rose 5% every year. Your particular expense can inflate faster or slower than the general price index. Use a conservative return assumption, then test a lower one. If you still come up short, the controllable levers are a higher contribution, a later date or a smaller goal.

Count emergency reserves separately from long-term investments. Money locked until retirement and a property you live in may make you wealthier on paper without helping with a sudden bill. The companion LRN guide covers pay, budgets, debt and insurance in more detail. Slow Rich

03 / THE MATH

Compounding needs time

At a steady 8%, ₹10,000 becomes ₹10,800 after a year and ₹11,664 after two. In year two you earn a return on the original ₹10,000 and on the previous ₹800 of growth.

Use total return to count both price changes and cash distributions. If you buy a share for ₹100, collect a ₹3 dividend and sell for ₹108, your total return is 11% before costs and tax. Quoted yield alone misses the move in price. For a fund comparison, check whether the benchmark includes reinvested dividends or interest.

Nominal return measures the number of rupees. Real return measures purchasing power. Divide one plus the nominal return by one plus inflation, then subtract one. An 8% nominal return against 5% inflation is about 2.86% real. Over many years that difference matters considerably.

Average return and compound return answer different questions

A portfolio that gains 50% and then loses 50% has a simple average return of zero. ₹100 becomes ₹150 and then ₹75. Its annual compound return is about −13.4%. CAGR, the compound annual growth rate, is the constant annual rate that connects a starting value to an ending value with no intervening cash flows.

If you add or withdraw money, XIRR uses the dates of those cash flows to estimate your personal annualized return. A fund’s time-weighted return chains together returns between cash flows to evaluate the investment independent of when an investor adds or removes money. Compare returns over the same dates, currency and cost basis. An annualized result from a lucky quarter can make a volatile investment look far more predictable than it is.

The rule of 72 is shorthand for a doubling time at a steady positive rate. Divide 72 by the rate as a percentage. At 8%, the shorthand gives nine years. Actual markets deliver uneven gains and losses, and fees and taxes change the result. SEC investing basics

04 / THE BUILDING BLOCKS

What can you actually own?

An asset class describes the exposure you own. A vehicle describes how you hold it. A stock fund and an individual stock are different vehicles for equity exposure. A bond ETF is still a bond investment.

Stocks give you a claim on a business

Companies can reinvest profits, pay dividends or buy back shares. Shareholders get what remains after obligations to creditors. They can lose their entire investment. When you buy on an exchange, you are usually buying from another investor. Buying new shares in an IPO or another issuance occurs in the primary market, with IPO proceeds going to the company for newly issued shares or to existing holders for shares they sell.

Market capitalization is share price multiplied by shares outstanding. A ₹100 share of a company with one billion shares represents a larger business by market cap than a ₹1,000 share of one with a million shares. Large-, mid- and small-cap describe company size relative to market conventions. Smaller companies can be less liquid and more vulnerable to funding trouble. A low unit price tells you little about valuation.

Bonds promise payments on a schedule

A ₹10,000 bond with a 7% annual coupon promises ₹700 a year under its terms. The issuer is expected to repay face value at maturity. Your yield depends on the price you pay. A bond bought above face value can still pay a 7% coupon on face value while delivering a lower yield to maturity. Yield to maturity is the discount rate that equates the price and scheduled payments. Earning that rate as a compounded return also depends on receiving those payments and reinvesting coupons at that rate.

When market yields rise, existing fixed coupons look less attractive, so fixed-rate bond prices generally fall. Duration estimates sensitivity to yield changes. Higher duration usually means more price movement for the same change in yield. Credit risk is the chance a borrower fails to pay. Bond funds add fund costs and changing holdings. A typical open-ended bond fund has no single maturity at which you automatically receive your original investment. SEC bond bulletin

Cash, real estate and everything else

Cash and equivalents include bank savings, short deposits and instruments such as Treasury bills. Compare maturity, access and who guarantees what. A money market or liquid mutual fund is a portfolio of securities whose value can change. In India, DICGC protection applies to eligible deposits at insured banks up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank in the same right and capacity, including principal and interest. DICGC deposit insurance

Property can generate rent and appreciation. Vacancy, repairs, taxes, debt, transaction costs and an exit that takes months can dominate the outcome. A REIT provides exposure to property through a trust or company. Listed units can trade more easily than a building, while remaining sensitive to leverage, property sectors and interest rates.

Gold and commodities can behave differently from stocks and bonds in some periods. Physical holdings may involve storage, insurance and wide dealer spreads. Funds can add fees and may own futures or mining shares, each of which behaves differently from the spot commodity. Gold has no ongoing cash flow and can fall or stagnate for long stretches.

Private equity, venture capital and private credit may involve long lockups, opaque marks, high fees, limited disclosures and manager risk. Infrequent price updates can make risk look lower. Cryptoassets can have extreme price moves, platform failures, irreversible transfers and regulatory or custody risk. Derivatives such as options and futures can hedge an existing exposure or amplify it. Margin, expiry and leverage make the path matter. SEC investment products

05 / WRAPPERS AND COSTS

Learn to read a fund

A fund pools investors’ money and buys assets according to a stated mandate. Owning a fund unit gives you proportional exposure to what the fund owns, after its costs.

Two decisions that often get confused
DecisionWhat it changes
Index or activeAn index fund follows published benchmark rules. An active manager can choose and weight holdings within a mandate. Either can hold stocks, bonds or other assets.
Mutual fund or ETFA conventional open-ended mutual fund usually transacts at the applicable NAV under cut-off rules. An ETF trades during exchange hours at a market price, with a bid–ask spread and possible premium or discount to NAV.

NAV is assets minus liabilities divided by the units outstanding. A ₹10 NAV and a ₹100 NAV can buy identical exposure for the same amount of money. Check whether an index is broad or narrow, how it selects and weights companies, and how often it reconstitutes. A market-cap-weighted index can concentrate in the largest companies. A sector ETF can hold dozens of names exposed to one economic shock. SEC fund mechanics

Read these before you buy

  1. Mandate and benchmark. What does it own? Check geography, sector, firm size, bond maturity and credit, and currency exposure. Read the benchmark methodology. For like-for-like performance, compare with the appropriate total-return benchmark.
  2. Holdings and overlap. Look at the largest positions and sector weights. A large-cap fund, a broad domestic index fund and a tech fund may each hold the same major technology names. Include employer shares when you count exposure.
  3. All-in cost. Add the relevant expense ratio, platform or advisory fees, trading commissions, spread, foreign exchange costs, exit load and taxes. Some apply once, others compound every year. Fund NAV returns generally reflect operating expenses already.
  4. Tracking and execution. Tracking difference is the gap between fund and index return over a period. Tracking error usually measures how variable that gap is. For an ETF, check spread, premium or discount, market hours and liquidity in the fund and underlying assets.
  5. Terms and risks. Read the factsheet, scheme documents or prospectus, portfolio, and risk label. Check purchase and redemption terms, cash distributions, any lock-in and how custody and reporting work.

Other fund designs you’ll encounter

A balanced or multi-asset fund holds more than one asset class and may rebalance for you. A target-date fund adjusts its mix along a scheduled glide path. Funds with the same date may take very different risks. A fund of funds holds other funds, so look through to the underlying exposures and all layers of cost. Some funds distribute cash. Others retain income or reinvest it under local terms. A payout generally reduces the fund’s NAV by the amount distributed, all else equal.

A fund that charges less keeps more of the same gross return in your pocket. When comparing funds with different mandates, weigh the exposures, taxes and trading costs too. SEC fee bulletin SEC target-date fund bulletin

06 / PORTFOLIO DESIGN

Build for bad years

Asset allocation is the mix of stocks, bonds, cash and other assets you hold. Start with how much damage you can survive and how long the money can stay invested.

Capacity

How much loss your finances can absorb. Consider job stability, emergency cash, debt, dependents and the deadline.

Tolerance

How much uncertainty you can live with without abandoning the plan. Think in rupees as well as percentages.

Need

How much growth a goal demands. A plan that needs extraordinary returns may require higher savings or a different date.

Volatility measures how much returns vary. Drawdown measures decline from a previous peak. A 40% loss turns ₹10 lakh into ₹6 lakh and then requires a 66.7% gain to get back to the peak. Permanent loss can result when a business fails, a borrower defaults or you have to sell at a bad time. Liquidity risk is the risk of needing money when you cannot sell promptly at a reasonable price.

Diversification spreads exposure across sources of risk, such as companies, sectors, countries, issuers and maturities. Correlation measures how returns move together and can change during crises. Stocks and bonds can fall in the same year. Global holdings may reduce dependence on one market while introducing currency, tax and access issues. Match cash and stabilizing assets to the currency and timing of near-term liabilities where practical. SEC allocation guide

Match the mix to the goal

Here are three illustrative allocations for different jobs. Percentages apply to that goal’s money. Emergency cash sits outside the example long-term portfolios. The bond sleeve’s maturity and credit quality can matter as much as its size.

A broad domestic stock index spreads company risk within one country. A global stock fund can spread country risk too. Read what it includes, what it excludes, and what it costs you to own.

07 / STRATEGIES

Find a strategy you can repeat

Each strategy needs a reason to exist, a way to measure it and a plan for when it looks foolish. Start with a broad, low-cost benchmark and make any added complexity earn its place.

Broad indexing and buy-and-hold

A broad index portfolio aims to capture the returns of a market or markets less costs. Choose the allocation first, build it with suitable low-cost funds and rebalance as needed. You’ll hold winners, losers and the occasional bubble along the way. Holding for years gives the investments time to work but leaves you exposed to market-wide losses.

William Sharpe’s arithmetic explains the cost hurdle. Within a fixed market, before costs, the average actively managed dollar and the average passively managed dollar earn the same return when taken across the whole market under his definitions. After costs, the higher-cost aggregate falls behind. S&P’s SPIVA reports compare active funds against assigned benchmarks and count funds that close or merge. Results vary by period and category, and a benchmark’s published return omits the costs of a product that tracks it. Sharpe’s paper SPIVA India scorecard

A first pass at valuing a business

Read the annual report. Explain in plain language what the business sells, who pays, why customers return and what could interrupt that. Compare profit with cash from operations. Inspect borrowing, interest coverage, capital spending, stock-based compensation and changes in share count. Review incentives, related-party transactions and risks that could change the economics.

P/E is share price divided by earnings per share. At ₹200 and ₹10 in earnings, it is 20×. Negative or cyclical earnings can make the number misleading. Free cash flow commonly means operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Discounted cash flow expresses future cash flows in present money using a discount rate. Small changes in long-run growth and discount rates can have an outsized effect. Compare cash flows with the matching valuation measure, such as equity value for equity cash flow.

A margin of safety, in Benjamin Graham’s sense, is a cushion between a conservative value estimate and the price. Use a range of plausible values and state what would invalidate your thesis. For example, if the investment only works with higher margins, cheaper financing and a generous exit multiple, each assumption deserves scrutiny. Compare the idea with the low-effort alternative of holding the market. The Intelligent Investor

Contribution schedule and market timing

A SIP is a systematic investment plan, a scheduled purchase often used for mutual funds in India. Investing as you earn puts new money to work regularly. If you already have a lump sum earmarked for a long-term portfolio, spreading purchases out involves choosing to hold some of it in cash. Vanguard found immediate investment beat a three-month phase-in about two-thirds of the time in one historical comparison. A short written phase-in can make sense if it helps you follow through. Neither approach protects you once the money is invested. Vanguard entry-timing research

A single backtest is a hypothesis generator. Check whether the rule was chosen after seeing the data, losing assets disappeared from the sample, trades used future information or costs were ignored. Ask how the strategy performed in regimes beyond the one that made its chart look good.

08 / FROM PLAN TO PURCHASE

Make your first investment

Pick the goal, choose the account, choose the asset, then place the order. That sequence keeps an app’s list of trending investments from choosing your portfolio for you.

  1. Write your instructions. Specify the purpose, date, contribution amount and target mix. Decide how you will act after a large loss. Keep near-term obligations and emergency money outside the risk budget.
  2. Choose an account. An ordinary brokerage account and a retirement account can hold similar assets but have different access, costs and tax rules. Compare local alternatives and available employer contributions. Check allowed holdings, withdrawal rules and beneficiary or nomination procedures.
  3. Verify the provider. Use the regulator or exchange’s own register. Review custody, exit or transfer charges, statements, account recovery and security. Use the verified app or site, a unique password and two-factor authentication if offered.
  4. Research the product. Record the exact scheme and plan, or the ticker, exchange and security identifier. Check benchmark, holdings, risks, full costs and liquidity. For a fund, read the factsheet and formal scheme documents or prospectus.
  5. Place and verify a small first order. Check the amount, units, price constraints and any charges. Review status, executed price and settlement. Save the confirmation and compare it with your statement. For recurring buys, confirm the debit and the subsequent allotment.

What happens when you click Buy?

A conventional open-ended mutual-fund purchase is generally specified by amount and executed at the applicable NAV under the relevant rules. For example, before fees or rounding, ₹5,000 at an applicable ₹50 NAV buys 100 units. The displayed NAV can be from the previous valuation. Cut-off time and receipt of funds can affect the NAV actually allotted.

For exchange trades, the bid is a price buyers offer and the ask is a price sellers request. If the best quotes are ₹99.90 and ₹100.10, the quoted spread is ₹0.20. A market order prioritizes execution, and the final price can be worse than the quote. A limit buy sets the most you will pay. It can fill in parts or stay unfilled. A stop order triggers another order once a threshold is reached. A gap may lead to an execution far from the trigger, or a stop-limit order may fail to execute. Confirm the order’s validity and avoid resubmitting while an order’s status is unclear. SEC order types

Getting started in India

For exchange-listed stocks and ETFs, you will typically use a bank account, a trading account with a registered broker and a demat account with a depository participant. For many mutual funds, you can invest in statement-of-account form through the AMC or an eligible platform after KYC without a demat account. Verify requirements for your route and residency. SEBI account guide SEBI KYC guide

For Indian mutual funds, Direct and Regular plans of the same scheme generally share a portfolio and investment objective. Regular plans include distribution expenses and generally have a higher expense ratio. Look at any separate charges for a platform or advice when comparing. Understand Growth and IDCW distribution options, exit loads, the scheme’s benchmark and the current SEBI Riskometer. Keep separate notes on whether a feature affects your return, your taxes or when you get cash. AMFI plan comparison SEBI Riskometer

For Indian government securities, RBI Retail Direct is another route for eligible investors. It gives access to eligible government issues and secondary trades. Credit backing and price stability are separate questions. Selling a longer-dated government bond before maturity exposes you to market yields and liquidity. RBI Retail Direct

For retirement in India, compare any employer-linked benefits with options such as the National Pension System and the Public Provident Fund. NPS is market-linked and its retirement account has rules for access and exit. PPF is a government small-savings scheme with a long term and restrictions on early access. Check eligibility, contribution rules, withdrawal terms and tax treatment before relying on either for a deadline. Count existing retirement holdings when deciding your overall mix. PFRDA on NPS India Post savings schemes

Tax depends on residency, asset type, account, dates and current rules. Capital gains, dividends and interest can follow different rules. A fund switch or sale to rebalance can create a taxable event. Keep your purchase cost, transaction dates, distribution records, statements and sales proceeds. Verify the rules for the relevant year and product before transacting. Indian Income Tax Department filing guidance

A first month, from salary to statement

Priya has an emergency fund and ₹12,000 left after ordinary expenses. She needs ₹72,000 for a course in a year, so she sets aside ₹6,000 a month in accessible savings. The other ₹6,000 is for retirement. She chooses an illustrative 70/30 stock and bond mix after testing a bad year. Her first scheduled contribution assigns ₹4,200 to a broad equity fund and ₹1,800 to a high-quality bond fund, subject to each fund’s minimums.

She checks the actual scheme names and applicable NAVs on the confirmations, makes sure both purchases have been allotted and records the costs. The next month’s prices will change how many units she gets. Six months later, if stock gains have pushed the equity weight above 70%, she can direct more of the next contribution to bonds. If the course starts early, she can access what she has saved for it and work out the remaining shortfall separately.

09 / THE LONG MIDDLE

Keep it on course

A portfolio is a set of decisions you make repeatedly. Write the rules while you are calm, and revisit them when your life changes.

Make a one-page investment policy

Write down the goal, target date, contribution, target weights, acceptable drift, eligible holdings and maximum position size for individual bets. Decide when to review the plan, who can access the records and how new contributions are directed. On a bad day, use the document to distinguish a change in circumstances from a change in prices.

An example policy

“This account funds spending starting in 25 years. I will contribute ₹10,000 a month when income allows. The target is 70% broad equity and 30% high-quality bonds. I will review every June, direct new cash to the underweight sleeve and consider trades if either weight is at least five percentage points off target, after checking tax and costs. If I need this money earlier, I will revisit the allocation.”

Rebalancing restores the mix you chose

If ₹60,000 of stock rises to ₹90,000 while ₹40,000 of bonds stays flat, stocks now make up approximately 69.2% of the ₹1.3 lakh portfolio. To return to 60/40, you could sell ₹12,000 of stocks and buy ₹12,000 of bonds, or add ₹20,000 to bonds alone. New contributions can spare you a sale. A calendar rule or a drift band gives you a consistent trigger. Taxes, loads and spreads may change the best route. SEC rebalancing guide

What changes when you start spending?

Withdrawals make the order of returns matter. Early losses can force sales when prices are low, leaving fewer units to recover later. This is sequence-of-returns risk. It can occur even with the same average and compound return on the underlying assets. Cash for near-term spending, a suitable allocation and flexible withdrawals can help manage the risk. Build a spending plan around essential costs, other income, inflation, longevity, taxes and the ability to adjust if markets disappoint.

Set a realistic review cadence. Check that contributions went through, the mandate still fits, fees did not creep up, and your mix still reflects your needs. If you are selling, identify the reason before placing the order. Spending on a planned goal, restoring a target mix or replacing a changed mandate are decisions you can explain without a market forecast.

10 / KEEPING YOUR EDGE

Get good at avoiding damage

Large, avoidable losses can undo years of careful saving. Make the expensive mistakes harder to commit in the first place.

Chasing last year’s winner
Ask what pushed returns up and what today’s price assumes. Compare against a relevant benchmark over different periods. Short histories and lists of survivors make success look easier to identify than it was.
Panic-selling your allocation
Check whether the goal, cash needs or investment itself changed. Price falls often feel like new information about your future. Use your written rules and distinguish a broad drawdown from a product or thesis failure.
Borrowing to invest
Margin adds interest expense and can force sales. A leveraged strategy can fail even if your long-term view turns out right. Some derivative positions can lose more than the initial capital posted. SEBI found that 93% of individual equity derivatives traders in its FY22–FY24 study incurred losses over the period. SEBI derivatives study
Confusing activity with skill
Record entry and exit reasons. Compare results with a suitable benchmark after costs and tax. A good decision can have a poor outcome, and a gamble can pay off. Use enough observations before claiming an edge.
Ignoring concentration outside the portfolio
Your paycheck, employer shares and home may all depend on the same company, city or economy. Assess how much of your livelihood and wealth rides on one outcome.
Trusting a screenshot over a register
Be wary of guaranteed high returns, pressure to act, fake dashboards, requests to transfer money to a personal account, and “unlock” fees before you can withdraw. Visit regulator and exchange sites independently. Never share an OTP or let a stranger control your device. Keep evidence and contact the provider, bank or appropriate official reporting channel promptly if you suspect fraud. SEBI scam guide SEBI grievance process

If you seek help, check a professional’s registration, specialty, compensation, conflicts of interest and services in writing. Ask how they are paid if you buy an investment, whether there are ongoing charges and how you can leave.

11 / THE READING ROOM

Good books, tested ideas

Read these for the problem each author helps you solve. Keep the principles and examine the assumptions behind any specific portfolio.

BENJAMIN GRAHAM · JASON ZWEIG

The Intelligent Investor

Graham separates the effort-intensive work of selecting securities from a defensive approach built for people with less time. His Mr. Market character gives you permission to ignore quotes that don’t suit you. Chapters 8 and 20 cover market fluctuations and margin of safety. Zweig’s commentary helps bridge eras.

Try it. Before buying a stock, write what makes it worth more than its price and what would prove you wrong.

HarperCollins edition

JOHN C. BOGLE

The Little Book of Common Sense Investing

Bogle makes the case for owning broad markets at low cost over long periods. Fees are a persistent drag even when returns are unpredictable. His case for indexing starts with the market and the price you pay to access it.

Try it. Compare the all-in cost and coverage of two funds targeting the same market.

Wiley edition

BURTON G. MALKIEL

A Random Walk Down Wall Street

Malkiel walks through bubbles, popular forecasting systems and the difficulty of beating competitive markets. He connects diversification with the investor’s time horizon and financial situation.

Try it. Ask what evidence could distinguish a new strategy from a chart selected after the fact.

W. W. Norton edition

MORGAN HOUSEL

The Psychology of Money

Housel puts luck, risk, endurance and room for error ahead of elaborate spreadsheets. A plan you can keep funding through setbacks can be more valuable than one with a more impressive assumed return.

Try it. Write how you would respond to a large decline while earning less than usual.

Harriman House edition

What the evidence can tell you

Warren Buffett on low-cost exposure. In Berkshire Hathaway’s 2013 letter, Buffett describes instructions for a particular trust to put 90% into a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund and 10% into short-term government bonds. Take the emphasis on simplicity and low friction. The trust’s equity-heavy U.S. allocation reflects its own circumstances. Berkshire’s letter, p. 19

Vanguard on what you can control. Set useful goals, choose a diversified balance, minimize costs and maintain discipline. These principles help organize decisions even when you change markets, account types or providers. Vanguard’s investing principles

S&P on active fund selection. For the decade ending December 2025, SPIVA India reported that about 76% of the Indian active large-cap equity funds studied trailed the assigned benchmark. One-year outcomes looked different across categories. Use the reported period, benchmark and fund universe to understand the claim. SPIVA India year-end 2025

Morningstar on investor behavior. Its U.S. Mind the Gap research compares fund returns with estimates of the returns earned by invested dollars, including the timing of flows. The gap illustrates why your own experience can differ from a fund chart. Cash flows have many causes, including ordinary spending needs. Morningstar Mind the Gap research

13 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Would you know what to do?

Five decisions you might face in the real world. Pick an answer, then check the reasoning.

1. Your tuition bill is due in 14 months. What matters most for that money?

With a fixed near-term deadline, liquidity and principal protection take priority. Even a broad stock ETF can fall sharply before the bill is due.

2. A ₹100 stock pays a ₹3 dividend and ends at ₹108. What’s your total return before costs?

Add ₹8 of price growth and ₹3 of cash income, then divide by the ₹100 initial price.

3. Market yields rise. What usually happens to an existing fixed-rate bond’s price?

New bonds offer more interest at the higher yield. The existing fixed payments become less attractive at the old price.

4. Your target is 60% stocks. A rally takes you to 70%. How could new contributions help?

Adding to the underweight assets can bring the stocks toward their target weight without realizing gains through a sale.

5. Two funds track the same index. The first has a lower NAV. What does that tell you?

NAV is a price per unit. Compare fund costs, tracking and holdings to assess the choice. A lower NAV lets the same amount buy more units.
