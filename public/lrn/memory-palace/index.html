The Memory Palace

A 2,500-year-old trick for remembering almost anything, by storing it inside a building you already know. What it is, whether it really works, and how to build your own, step by step.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 40 min · 11 interactives

Step inside

Around 500 BC, so the story goes, a Greek poet named Simonides of Ceos was hired to recite a poem at a banquet. Halfway through the evening, a messenger called him outside. While he was gone, the roof of the banquet hall collapsed, crushing everyone inside so badly that their families couldn't recognise them.

Simonides could. He closed his eyes, pictured the hall, and walked around the table in his mind, seeing who had been sitting where. One by one, he named every guest.

The Roman orator Cicero told that story four centuries later, and said it taught Simonides something important: memory loves places. If you want to remember things in order, put them in locations you know, and walk past them in your mind. Greek and Roman speakers used it to deliver hours-long speeches without notes. Medieval monks used it to memorise entire books. Today, people at memory championships use it to memorise a shuffled deck of cards in well under a minute.

They call it the method of loci. Most people call it a memory palace.

Here's the good news: you already own one. Close your eyes and picture your home. Walk from the front door to the kitchen. Notice where the shoes go, the sofa, the switchboard, the smell of the kitchen. You can do it effortlessly, in detail, without ever having tried to memorise it. That ability, your brain's extraordinary memory for places, is the engine of the whole technique.

This guide explains how it works in plain language, looks honestly at the science of whether it does, and then walks you through building your first palace. By the end, you'll have memorised a shopping list you'll struggle to forget, and you'll have the tools to memorise speeches, vocabulary, numbers and names.

I

What is a memory palace?

A place you know, a route through it, and strange pictures left along the way.

Before we explain anything, let's measure your memory as it is today. It takes one minute. You'll come back to this score later.

Most people remember somewhere between five and seven of those ten words, usually the first few and the last few. That's not a failing. Lists of unconnected words are exactly the kind of thing human memory is bad at. Nothing ties them together, so they slip away.

A memory palace fixes that by giving every item somewhere to live. It has three ingredients:

01

A place

Somewhere you know extremely well: your home, your school, your office, the route to the market. You need to be able to walk it in your mind without effort.

02

A route

A fixed path through that place, with specific stopping points called loci (Latin for “places”; one is a locus): the door, the sofa, the TV, the table. Always walked in the same order.

03

Vivid images

Each thing you want to remember becomes a bizarre, exaggerated, moving picture, placed at one locus and interacting with it. The stranger the image, the better it sticks.

To remember, you simply take a mental walk along the route. As you arrive at each locus, the image you left there pops back into your head, and with it, the item. Because the route has an order, your list comes back in order too, forwards or backwards, and you can even jump straight to the seventh item.

Why it works: your brain is a navigator

For most of human history, remembering where things were (water, food, danger, the way home) was a matter of life and death. Remembering lists of abstract words never was. So our brains evolved to be superb at spatial and visual memory, and fairly poor at everything else.

Neuroscience backs this up. Deep in the brain, a seahorse-shaped structure called the hippocampus is central to both memory and navigation. In 2014, the Nobel Prize in medicine went to John O'Keefe and May-Britt and Edvard Moser for discovering “place cells” and “grid cells”, neurons that act like an internal map and GPS. A memory palace hijacks that map, piggybacking boring information onto a system that's brilliant at storing places.

Pictures help too. In a famous 1973 experiment, the psychologist Lionel Standing showed people 10,000 photographs, a few seconds each, over five days. Afterwards, they could pick out which pictures they'd seen with around 83 percent accuracy. Psychologists call this the picture superiority effect: images are remembered far better than words. A memory palace combines both superpowers: vivid pictures, stored in familiar places.

You're not improving your memory. You're translating information into the language your memory already speaks.

II

Twenty-five centuries of palaces

From Greek orators to medieval monks, Jesuit missionaries and modern memory athletes.

Before cheap paper and printing, a trained memory wasn't a party trick; it was an essential professional skill for lawyers, priests, scholars and politicians. Drag or scroll through the timeline.

In other cultures, similar ideas developed independently. The Australian writer and researcher Lynne Kelly has argued that many oral cultures, including Aboriginal Australians with their songlines, have long stored vast amounts of knowledge by linking it to features of the landscape, sung and walked in sequence. India's own oral traditions preserved the Vedas with astonishing accuracy for thousands of years, using intricate recitation patterns rather than locations: a reminder that there's more than one way to build a remarkable memory.

III

Does it actually work?

Yes, remarkably well, for the right kind of job. Here's what the research says, and what it doesn't.

The method of loci is one of the most studied memory techniques in psychology, and the overall verdict is clear: it reliably improves recall of ordered lists, often dramatically. A 2021 meta-analysis by Twomey and Kroneisen, pooling decades of experiments, and a 2025 systematic review both concluded it is effective for adults. Here are four studies worth knowing. Tap each to see the result.

The honest caveats

  • It's best for ordered lists of discrete things. Speeches, sequences, steps, vocabulary, names, numbers, the planets, the bones of the hand. It's much less useful for understanding a concept or learning a skill.
  • It takes effort up front. Creating good images is slower than reading a list once. The payoff comes when you need to remember for days, weeks or years, or when the list is long.
  • It takes practice. In studies, people who've just learned the method improve a lot; people who've practised for weeks improve far more. Your first palace will feel clumsy. Your fifth won't.
  • It's a memory tool, not an understanding tool. Memorising the steps of the Krebs cycle isn't the same as understanding it. The best students combine memory palaces with explaining ideas in their own words, practice questions and spaced review.
  • Not everyone pictures things the same way. People with aphantasia (little or no mental imagery) can still use the method, leaning on spatial layout, words, sounds and stories instead of pictures. More on that at the end.

IV

Build your first palace

Seven steps. We'll do them together, using an ordinary Indian flat and a shopping list.

We'll memorise a ten-item shopping list: tomatoes, paneer, bread, eggs, bananas, toothpaste, batteries, rice, coriander, detergent. Don't try to memorise it now. Just read the steps.

  1. 1

    Choose a place you know by heart

    Start with your own home. You need to be able to walk through it mentally, effortlessly, seeing details. Bigger isn't better; familiar is. Your childhood home, your hostel, your office and your daily walk all make great palaces later.

  2. 2

    Plan a route, and never change it

    Decide on a path, say, in through the front door, clockwise around the living room, into the kitchen, then the bathroom, bedroom and balcony. Always walk it in the same direction. The order of the route becomes the order of your list.

  3. 3

    Pick your loci

    Choose specific, distinct stopping points along the route: the shoe rack, the sofa, the TV, the table. Make them different from each other (five identical chairs will blur together), spaced a little apart, and well lit in your imagination. Start with ten.

  4. 4

    Turn each item into a concrete picture

    Tomatoes are easy: picture tomatoes. Abstract things need a stand-in: “democracy” might become a ballot box, the name “Sunil” might become a sun shining on a nail. We'll cover tricks for numbers and words in the next chapter.

  5. 5

    Make the picture ridiculous

    This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters most. A tomato sitting on your doormat is forgettable. Giant tomatoes exploding against the door as you open it, splattering juice into your chappals, is not. Try the image maker below.

  6. 6

    Make it interact with the locus

    Don't just put the image near the spot. Make it do something to the spot: crush it, melt it, burst out of it, use it. The interaction glues the image to the place.

  7. 7

    Walk it back, then review

    Walk the route once or twice right away, recalling each image. Then review it later today, tomorrow, and in a few days. Each walk makes the memory stronger and faster to retrieve.

Step 5, the secret ingredient: make it unforgettable

Our memories evolved to keep what's surprising, emotional, funny, disgusting or dangerous, and to throw away what's ordinary. Memory teachers have lists of principles for making images stick; here are the six that matter most. Take a boring item, a carton of milk, and switch them on one by one.

V

Turning anything into a picture

Words, names, foreign vocabulary and numbers, made concrete.

Shopping lists are easy because the items are already things you can see. Most of what you'll want to remember isn't. The skill of memory palaces is really the skill of turning the abstract into the concrete. There are four main tricks.

  • Sounds-like. Break a word or name into sound-alike pictures. “Priyanka” might become a pre-paid yank on a rope; “Mitochondria” a mighty kite stuck in a condo. It doesn't have to be perfect; it just has to point you back to the word.
  • Symbols. Represent an idea with something that stands for it: justice becomes scales, the French Revolution a guillotine, inflation a balloon.
  • Foreign words: the keyword method. Find an English (or Hindi) word that sounds like the foreign word, and link it to the meaning. The Spanish pato (duck) sounds like “pot”: picture a duck wearing a pot as a helmet. In 1975, Richard Atkinson and Michael Raugh found that students learning Russian vocabulary this way remembered substantially more than students using rote repetition.
  • Names and faces. Find a striking feature of the face, turn the name into an image, and connect them. Mr Patel with big eyebrows: picture a petal growing out of each eyebrow.

Numbers: the Major System

Numbers are the hardest things to remember because they mean nothing. The classic solution, around since the 1600s and refined since, is the Major System: give each digit a consonant sound, then add vowels freely to make words. Once you know the code, any number becomes a picture.

Memory athletes go further. Many learn a fixed image for every number from 00 to 99 (like the list above) and then a Person–Action–Object system, where each card or number pair has a person, an action and an object, so six digits compress into one scene: Shah Rukh Khan (person) juggling (action) a pressure cooker (object). That's how a shuffled deck of 52 cards becomes about eighteen scenes, placed along a single palace route. You don't need any of this to start. But it shows how far the idea scales.

VI

Making it last

The forgetting curve, and the review schedule that beats it.

In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus spent years memorising lists of nonsense syllables and testing himself, to measure how quickly memories fade. The result is the forgetting curve: without review, we lose a large chunk of new information within a day, and then the loss slows down.

A memory palace makes the curve much flatter, because vivid images in familiar places are stickier. But the real trick is spaced review: walking the palace again just as you're about to forget. Each review resets the curve and makes it shallower, so the gaps between reviews can get longer and longer. Add reviews below and watch.

The curves here are schematic, not data from one study; real forgetting depends on the material and the person. But the shape is well established. A good simple schedule for a new palace: walk it right away, later the same day, the next day, after three days, after a week, and after a month. After that, an occasional walk keeps it alive for years.

Reviews work best as active recall: walk the route and try to retrieve each image before checking, rather than rereading your notes. Retrieval itself strengthens memory, a finding psychologists call the testing effect.

VII

What to use it for

Where a memory palace shines, where it's overkill, and where it won't help at all.

Giving a speech without notes

This is the original use. Don't memorise the speech word for word; memorise its key points. Turn each main idea into an image and place it at a locus. Opening joke at the front door, the problem on the sofa, your first argument on the TV, the story about your grandmother at the dining table. Then, while you speak, you simply walk the house. Some historians think the English phrases “in the first place” and “in the second place” are fossils of this ancient habit.

Studying for exams

Use palaces for the parts of a subject that really are lists: the order of reactions, the causes of a war, the cranial nerves, Articles of the Constitution, the names of the Mughal emperors in order. Give each subject its own palace (your school for history, your college for biology), so lists don't collide. Combine it with practice questions and explaining concepts aloud for the understanding part.
