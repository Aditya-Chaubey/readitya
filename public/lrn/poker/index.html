All In

A friendly guide to poker: how the world's most famous card game works, why it's really about people, and how to play your first hand without anyone noticing it's your first hand.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 30 min · 11 interactives · 1 playable hand

Shuffle up and deal ♠

Here is the strangest thing about poker: you can lose holding the best cards, and win holding nothing at all. In chess the better position usually wins. In poker the better story often does.

That's because poker isn't really a card game. It's a game about decisions made with incomplete information, played with cards. You see some of the picture, your opponents see a different part of it, and everyone has to guess the rest, while putting something they care about on the line.

This guide takes you from "which hand beats which?" to sitting down at a friendly game and holding your own. We'll meet the deck, learn the hand rankings, walk through a full hand of Texas Hold'em, and get a feel for odds, bluffing and table manners. At the end, you'll play a hand against a cat.

House rule: this is a guide to the game. Play with friends for chips, matchsticks or bragging rights. If you ever play for money, set a limit before you sit down and stick to it. The cards don't care how much you've lost.
1

Chapter one

Fifty-two cards

The only equipment you need, and one very large number.

Poker uses a standard deck: 52 cards in four suits (♠ spades, ♥ hearts, ♦ diamonds, ♣ clubs) and thirteen ranks, from 2 up through 10, then Jack, Queen, King and Ace. No jokers.

In most forms of poker, suits are all equal: no suit beats another. Only rank matters, except when five cards share a suit, which is a flush. The Ace is the most flexible card in the deck: it's the highest card, but it can also play low, below the 2, to make the smallest straight, A-2-3-4-5.

Every time you shuffle properly, you are almost certainly holding an arrangement of cards that has never existed before in the history of the universe. There are 52 × 51 × 50 × … × 1 ways to order a deck. That number, written 52!, is about 8 followed by 67 zeros. If everyone who has ever lived had shuffled a deck every second since the Big Bang, they wouldn't have scratched the surface.

So no: you haven't "seen this hand before".

Chapter two

What beats what

Ten hands. Memorize these and you can play.

A poker hand is always exactly five cards. The rarer the hand, the higher it ranks. That's the whole logic, and it means the order isn't arbitrary: it falls straight out of the math. Click through the ladder, from the hand you'll see most often to the one you may never see at all.

A few rules settle the arguments that will absolutely come up:

  • Same hand type? Compare the important cards first. A pair of Kings beats a pair of Queens. If the pairs match, compare the leftover cards, called kickers, one at a time from the top.
  • Suits never break ties. Two identical straights in different suits split the pot.
  • Full house vs full house: the three-of-a-kind part decides. K-K-K-2-2 beats Q-Q-Q-A-A.
  • The wheel: A-2-3-4-5 is the lowest straight. Straights don't "wrap": Q-K-A-2-3 is nothing.

Think you've got it? Here's a quick test. Pick the winner.

Chapter three

Anatomy of a hand

Texas Hold'em, from shuffle to showdown.

There are dozens of kinds of poker, but one rules them all: Texas Hold'em. It's what you see on TV and in most home games. Each player gets two private cards, five shared cards are dealt face up in the middle, and you make the best five-card hand you can from all seven. Scroll to play through a real hand, seat by seat.

The setup

Six players, one deck, a pile of chips each. Everyone starts with 200 chips. You're at the bottom.

The button

The white disc is the dealer button. Eli has it this hand. It marks who acts last in each round of betting, which is a big advantage, and it moves one seat to the left after every hand so everyone gets a turn.

The blinds

Before any cards are dealt, the two players to the button's left post forced bets called blinds: the small blind (1 chip, that's you) and the big blind (2 chips, Ana). Without them, everyone could wait for perfect cards forever. Blinds make sure there's always something to fight over.

Hole cards

The dealer gives everyone two cards face down, one at a time, starting from the small blind. These are your hole cards, and only you get to see them. You peek: A♠ Q♠. That's a strong start: two high cards of the same suit.

Pre-flop betting

Betting starts with the player left of the big blind. Bo looks at 7♣ 2♦, the worst starting hand in Hold'em, and folds. Cy calls 2. Dee folds. Eli raises to 6. You call 6. Ana folds her big blind. Cy calls. Three players remain.

The flop

The bets are swept into the pot: 20 chips. The dealer "burns" a card (discards it face down, to stop cheating) and deals three cards face up in the middle: the flop. Q♦ 9♠ 4♠. You now have a pair of Queens with an Ace kicker, and four spades: one more spade makes a flush.

Betting on the flop

After the flop, betting starts with the first player still in the hand to the left of the button. That's you. You check (pass without betting). Cy checks. Eli bets 10. You call. Cy, hoping to make a straight with J-10, calls too.

The turn

Burn one, deal one: the fourth community card, called the turn, is 2♥. A blank: it helps nobody. You check, Cy checks, Eli bets 20. You call. Cy gives up and folds.

The river

Burn one, deal the last card, the river: K♠. A fifth spade! You have a flush, and with the Ace, the best possible flush. You bet 40. Eli thinks for a long time… and calls.

Showdown

When the last bet is called, the remaining players reveal their cards: the showdown. Eli turns over 8♥ 8♣, a pair of eights. You show your ace-high flush, using both your cards and three spades from the board. Each player uses the best five of their seven cards.

Ship it

The 170-chip pot slides your way. The button moves one seat to the left, onto you, the blinds move with it, and a new hand begins. That's it. That's poker. Everything else is detail and nerve.

Chapter four

Five things you can do

Every decision in poker is one of these.

When it's your turn, the options depend on one question: has anyone bet yet in this round?

Check

If nobody has bet yet

Pass the action along without putting chips in. Tap the table twice to signal it, the most satisfying gesture in poker.

Bet

If nobody has bet yet

Put chips in first. Now everyone else has to match it, raise it, or leave.

=

Call

If someone has bet

Match the current bet to stay in the hand.

↑

Raise

If someone has bet

Match the bet and increase it. Everyone else must now match your new amount.

Fold

Any time it's your turn

Give up your cards and any claim to the pot. The chips you've already put in stay there.

A round of betting ends when everyone still in the hand has put in the same amount (or everyone has checked). If everyone folds except one player, that player wins the pot immediately, without showing their cards. This is why bluffing works.

How much can you bet?

That depends on the game's betting structure. In No-Limit, you can bet any amount up to everything in front of you, the famous "all in". In Pot-Limit, the most you can bet is the size of the pot. In Fixed-Limit, bets come in set sizes. No-Limit Hold'em is the most popular game in the world, and also the most dramatic. A player who runs out of chips mid-hand isn't out of luck: they can still win the part of the pot they matched, and any extra bets go into a separate side pot.

Chapter five

Best five of seven

Reading the board is a skill. Practice it here.

In Hold'em you always have seven cards to choose from, two in your hand and five on the board, and your hand is the best five you can make. You can use both of your hole cards, one, or even none: if the board itself is the best hand, you "play the board" and probably split the pot.

Beginners misread hands all the time. Overlooking a straight on the board or forgetting that everyone shares the same flush is a classic. Deal some hands and see how the best five light up.

Chapter six

A little bit of math

Outs, odds, and why good players are calculators in hoodies.

Poker is a game of skill because it's a game of chance. Over one hand, anyone can win. Over thousands of hands, the player who makes better decisions about probability wins. You don't need much math, just two ideas.

Idea one: count your outs

An out is a card that would improve your hand to a winner. Remember our flush draw after the flop? There are 13 spades in the deck; you could see four of them, so 9 spades were left: 9 outs. To turn outs into a percentage there's a famous shortcut, the rule of 2 and 4: on the flop, multiply your outs by 4 to estimate your chance of hitting by the river; on the turn, multiply by 2.

Idea two: pot odds

Say the pot holds 80 chips and your opponent bets 20. To call you must risk 20 to win 100 (the pot plus their bet). You're getting 100-to-20, or 5-to-1, so you need to win more than 1 time in 6, about 17%, for the call to pay off in the long run. If your outs give you better than that, call. If not, fold, even if it hurts.

Here's a third tool, the one the pros use: equity, your share of the pot if all the cards were dealt out right now. Pick a classic matchup and the page will deal it out thousands of times.

Chapter seven

How to not be the fish

Starting hands, position, bluffs and tells.

There's an old saying: if you can't spot the sucker at the table within half an hour, it's you. Poker players call that player the fish. Here's how to swim.

1. Fold more

The single biggest beginner mistake is playing too many hands. There are 169 different starting hands in Hold'em, and most of them lose money. Good players fold the majority of the time before the flop and play their good hands aggressively. That style has a name: tight-aggressive.

2. Position is power

Acting last is a huge advantage: you get to see what everyone else does before you decide. That's why the button is the best seat at the table and why players near it can play more hands. In early position, with lots of players still to act behind you, play only your strongest hands.

3. Bluff with a reason

A bluff is a bet with a weak hand, designed to make better hands fold. Movies make it look like poker is all bluffing. It isn't. A good bluff tells a believable story: your bets on earlier streets should make sense with the strong hand you're pretending to have. Bluffing works best against one or two opponents, and very badly against players who call everything. Never try to bluff someone who isn't paying attention.

The cousin of the bluff is the semi-bluff: betting with a hand that isn't good yet but could become good, like our flush draw. You win if they fold, and you can still win if they call and the spade comes.

The long stare

People acting strong are often weak, and vice versa. A player who stares you down may be trying to scare you off.

Shaky hands

Counterintuitive: trembling when betting often signals a strong hand. Adrenaline is hard to fake.

The chip glance

A quick look at their chips after the flop can mean someone liked what they saw and is planning a bet.

Timing

Online, there are no faces, but how fast someone acts is a tell too. An instant call often means a medium-strength hand.

Tells are fun but unreliable. Betting patterns, what someone does over many hands, are far more trustworthy than a twitch.

Chapter nine

From riverboats to robots

Two hundred years of bluffing.

Nobody knows exactly where poker came from. It probably descends from several older bluffing games: the French poque, the German pochen, perhaps the Persian as-nas. What we do know is that it grew up in America, in the early 1800s, and travelled the way Americans did.

    The family tree

    Hold'em is the most popular, but it's one branch of a big family. Flip the cards to meet the others.

    Chapter ten

    Don't be that player

    Table manners, and the words you'll hear.

    ♠

    Act in turn

    Wait until it's your turn to fold, bet or even react. Acting early gives away information and annoys everyone.

    ♥

    Say it, then do it

    Announce "call" or "raise to 20" out loud. Dropping in some chips, then reaching back for more, is a string bet and usually not allowed.

    ♦

    Don't splash the pot

    Place your bet in front of you, not thrown into the pile, so everyone can see how much you put in.

    ♣

    Don't slow-roll

    If you have the winning hand at showdown, show it. Making someone think they've won before revealing yours is the rudest move in poker.

    ♠

    Protect your cards

    Keep a chip or your hand on your cards. If the dealer mucks them by accident, they're gone.

    ♥

    Be a good loser (and winner)

    Bad beats happen to everyone. Nobody wants to hear the story of yours. Win quietly, lose gracefully.

    Speak poker

    Poker rewards a particular kind of thinking: making the best decision you can with what you know, then letting go of the result. You'll make good calls and lose, and bad calls and win. Over enough hands the luck evens out, and what's left is how well you decided.

    That's not a bad lesson for a card game to teach. Find some friends, a deck and a pile of something to bet with. Fold more than feels natural. And when you finally get that flush on the river, try to keep a straight face.
