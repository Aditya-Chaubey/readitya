lrn · No. 06 · Style & manners

Well Suited

How to dress, and behave, like a gentleman in the modern age. A field guide to fit, fabric, shoes, dress codes and the small courtesies that matter more than any of it.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 40 min · 12 interactives

Get dressed

The word "gentleman" used to mean something narrow: a man with land and a family crest who didn't have to work. Thankfully, that meaning died long ago. What survived is better. Today a gentleman is simply someone who takes care: with how he looks, with how he treats people, with the small things most people don't bother with.

This is a guide to both halves of that. The clothes, because they're the first thing anyone knows about you, and getting them right is easier than you think. And the manners, because a perfect suit on a rude man is still a rude man.

You won't need much money. You'll need to learn a handful of rules, learn which ones you can break, and find a good tailor.

Along the way there's a gentleman drawn entirely in code, who'll model everything. You can adjust his fit, fasten and unfasten his buttons, mix his patterns, dress him for six dress codes and build his outfits yourself. He's very patient.

I

Chapter one

What makes a gentleman, anyway?

Five ideas that sit underneath every rule in this guide.

Read enough style guides and you'll notice they disagree about almost everything: lapel widths, trouser rises, whether brown shoes are allowed in the city. But the people who've thought hardest about it, from tailors on Savile Row to writers like Alan Flusser and Hardy Amies, keep landing on the same few principles. Learn these and you can work out most of the rules yourself.

01

Fit

A cheap suit that fits will always beat an expensive one that doesn't. Fit is ninety percent of looking good, and it's the part most men skip.

02

Restraint

The best-dressed man in the room is rarely the loudest. One interesting thing per outfit is plenty. Let the rest be quietly excellent.

03

Appropriateness

Dressing well means dressing for the occasion and the people in it. Overdressing can be as rude as underdressing: it makes the host feel awkward.

04

Care

Polished shoes, pressed shirts, clean nails, a recent haircut. Taking care of things is visible, and it reads as respect: for yourself and for others.

05

Consideration

All manners come down to one idea: make the people around you comfortable. Clothes are just the first, most visible way of doing it.

"A man should look as if he had bought his clothes with intelligence, put them on with care, and then forgotten all about them."

Hardy Amies, tailor to Queen Elizabeth II

That last bit, forgotten all about them, is the goal. The Italians call it sprezzatura: a studied carelessness that makes looking good seem effortless. You get there by sweating the details first, so you never have to think about them again.

II

Chapter two

Fit is everything

Six checkpoints that separate "wearing a suit" from "being worn by one".

Off-the-rack clothes are cut for an imaginary average man who doesn't exist. Almost everyone needs some adjustment, and almost nobody gets it. Here's what to look for, in order of importance. Drag the sliders to mess up our gentleman's suit, then see if you can fix it.

What a tailor can (and can't) fix

A good alterations tailor is the best-value relationship in menswear. Find one before you need one, and budget a little extra on top of anything you buy. But know the limits: some things are a quick job, and some aren't worth trying.

Easy & cheap

  • Hemming trousers
  • Tapering trouser legs
  • Shortening sleeves (with non-working buttons)
  • Taking in a shirt's sides
  • Taking in a trouser waist

Doable, costs more

  • Taking in a jacket's waist
  • Shortening sleeves with working buttonholes
  • Fixing a gap behind the collar
  • Letting out (only if there's spare cloth in the seams)

Walk away

  • Shoulders too wide or narrow
  • Jacket much too short or long
  • Changing lapel width
  • Anything more than about one size
Shopping rule of thumb: buy for your shoulders and chest, then let a tailor do the rest. If a jacket fits there, almost everything else can be made right.

III

Chapter three

The suit, decoded

The most useful garment a man can own, part by part.

The modern suit is about 150 years old and has barely changed. It's a kind of uniform that flatters almost every body by doing three things: broadening the shoulders, defining the waist and lengthening the legs. Every detail serves one of those goals. Tap the numbers to learn the vocabulary.

The button rule: Sometimes, Always, Never

This is the single most-broken rule in men's clothing, and the easiest to get right. Try fastening and unfastening the buttons below. Then sit him down.

Your first suit

If you're buying one suit to cover interviews, weddings, funerals and the occasional big meeting, the advice is nearly unanimous:

Colour

Navy or charcoal

Both work day and night, at happy and sad occasions, and with every shirt and tie. Black is for evening and funerals only; it looks harsh in daylight.

Cloth

Mid-weight wool

A worsted wool around 260–300 g/m² (9–10 oz) handles most of the year. Avoid shiny synthetic blends: they don't breathe and look cheap fast.

Cut

Two-button, notch lapel

Single-breasted, with medium lapels and flap pockets. One or two vents at the back. Nothing trendy, so it won't date.

Budget

Spend on fit

A mid-priced suit plus good tailoring beats a designer suit worn straight off the hanger, every single time.

Once you have the essentials, the cloth is where it gets fun. A suit's fabric changes its personality more than its cut does.

Worsted

Smooth, crisp, the business default. Holds a crease well.

Flannel

Soft, brushed, slightly fuzzy wool. Warm and wonderfully relaxed in autumn and winter.

Tweed

Thick, rough, country-born wool. Perfect for sport coats, not boardrooms.

Linen

Cool and breezy for hot weather. It wrinkles, and that's part of the charm.

Seersucker

Puckered cotton stripes that stand off the skin. The classic American summer suit.

Pinstripe

Thin vertical lines that make you look taller. Traditional, quite "City banker".

IV

Chapter four

Shirts, ties and the space between

Everything people see when they look you in the eye.

The area between your chin and your jacket button is the most-looked-at part of any outfit, because it's right below your face. Small choices there make a big difference.

The shirt

A dress shirt should fit close without pulling. You should be able to slip two fingers inside the buttoned collar. The shoulder seam sits on your shoulder bone, the cuff covers your wrist bone, and there's no big muffin-top of fabric at the waist when it's tucked in.

Start with white and light blue: between them they handle 90% of situations. Then add an oxford button-down (the most versatile shirt ever made, good with a suit or jeans), and maybe a subtle stripe or a pale pink. Short-sleeved dress shirts with a tie are, sadly, never a good idea.

The tie

Three rules cover nearly everything. The tip touches your belt buckle: not above, not below. The width roughly matches your lapels, so about 7–9 cm at the widest point for most men. And always pinch a little dimple just under the knot as you tighten it. It's the difference between a tie that looks tied and one that looks strangled.

Silk ties are for business. Knit ties (square-ended, textured) are more relaxed and great with sport coats. Wool and cashmere ties are for autumn tweeds. And a dark tie with a small, regular pattern, like dots or a fine stripe, works with almost any shirt.

The pocket square

The cheapest way to show you put thought in. It shouldn't match your tie exactly; it should pick up a colour from somewhere in the outfit. A white linen square goes with everything. Hover over each one to see it pop up.

Presidential

A neat, flat strip of white. Formal, minimal, impossible to get wrong. Best for business and black tie.

One point

A single triangle peak. A touch more personality, still crisp.

Crown

Three or four peaks. Showy, a bit old-school, good for weddings and parties.

Puff

Pinch the middle, stuff it in, let it bloom. Relaxed and great for silk or patterned squares.

V

Chapter five

Colour, pattern, and how not to clash

A system simple enough to use while half-awake at 7 am.

Classic menswear uses a surprisingly small palette. Build almost everything from a few neutrals, then add one accent at a time.

The foundation

NavyCharcoalMid greyCamelBrownKhakiWhite

The accents

BurgundyForestOliveRustSkyPinkMustard

Two more ideas make colour easy. First, contrast should suit your complexion. If your hair and skin are very different in tone (dark hair, fair skin, say), you can wear high contrast: navy and white. If they're similar, softer combinations like grey and light blue tend to be more flattering. Second, darker on the bottom, lighter on top is a reliable default. It draws the eye up to your face.

Mixing patterns

Pattern-mixing scares people, but it runs on one rule: vary the scale. If two patterns are the same size, they fight. If one is clearly bigger, the eye reads them as separate things. Try it.

VI

Chapter six

From the ground up

People look at your shoes. They really do.

Shoes are the quiet giveaway. A sharp suit with scuffed, square-toed shoes falls apart; jeans and a T-shirt with good leather shoes look surprisingly considered. Here's the classic ladder, from most formal to most relaxed.

    The leather rules

    Rule 01

    Belt matches shoes

    Same colour family and roughly the same finish. Brown suede shoes, brown suede-ish belt. Black shiny shoes, black smooth belt. Your watch strap can join in too.

    Rule 02

    Socks match trousers

    Matching socks to trousers makes your legs look longer. Patterned socks are fine if they pick up a colour from the outfit. Never white athletic socks with dress shoes.

    Rule 03

    Socks go over the calf

    When you sit and cross your legs, nobody should see your shin. Over-the-calf socks fix this, and stay up all day.

    Rule 04

    Use shoe trees

    Cedar shoe trees soak up moisture and keep the shape. Rest leather shoes a day between wears and they'll last ten years or more.

    Myth check: "no brown in town." An old British rule said brown shoes were only for the countryside. It's long dead. Dark brown is fine with navy and grey suits for almost everything except black tie, funerals and the most conservative offices.

    VII

    Chapter seven

    Dress codes, decoded

    What the invitation actually means, from most formal to least.

    Formality

    01 · The rarest

    White tie

    The most formal dress code there is: state banquets, royal events, a handful of grand balls. A black tailcoat that's cut short at the front, a white piqué waistcoat, a stiff white wing-collar shirt and a white bow tie. Patent shoes. If you're invited, you'll probably rent, and that's completely fine.

    Rule: the waistcoat must never peek below the front of the tailcoat.

    02 · Evening formal

    Black tie

    Galas, formal weddings after 6 pm, award dinners. A black or midnight-blue dinner jacket (a "tuxedo" in America) with satin peak or shawl lapels, matching trousers with a satin stripe, a white dress shirt, a black bow tie and black shoes. No notch lapels, no long ties, no exceptions.

    "Black tie optional" means a dinner jacket is welcome, but a dark suit and tie is fine.

    03 · Lounge suit

    Business formal

    Interviews, important meetings, most weddings, funerals, the law courts. A dark suit, in navy or charcoal; a white or light-blue shirt; a conservative tie; black or dark-brown oxfords. Also called "lounge suit" or, on invitations, sometimes "cocktail attire".

    For a funeral: the darkest suit you own, white shirt, dark tie, black shoes.

    04 · The office

    Business casual

    The most confusing code, because it means different things in different offices. Safest reading: a suit with pieces removed. A blazer or sport coat (optional), collared shirt, no tie needed, wool trousers or chinos, leather shoes. No jeans unless you see the boss in them.

    When in doubt, wear the blazer. You can always take it off.

    05 · Out and about

    Smart casual

    Dinners out, parties, dates, gallery openings. This is where personal style lives: a textured sport coat or a good knit, an open collar, dark jeans or chinos, boots or loafers. Polished, but relaxed. Like you made an effort without trying too hard.

    The secret ingredient is texture: tweed, suede, knitwear, flannel.

    06 · Off duty

    Casual

    Weekends, barbecues, travel. Casual doesn't mean careless. A polo or a good T-shirt that fits, chinos or jeans that aren't faded or torn, clean sneakers. Ironed, clean, fitting well. That's the whole trick.

    Shorts: above the knee, not cargo, and never with dress shoes.

    IX

    Chapter nine

    Now you dress him

    Put it all together, and see how few clothes you actually need.

    Every piece of clothing has a formality level, from a dinner jacket at the top to a T-shirt at the bottom. The whole skill of putting outfits together is keeping your pieces close together on that scale. A dinner jacket with sneakers is jarring; a blazer with jeans works because they're only a few steps apart. Build some outfits and see what the tailor thinks.

    The capsule wardrobe

    Here's the secret that saves money and morning stress: a small wardrobe of pieces that all go together creates far more outfits than a big wardrobe of things that don't. Navy, grey, white, blue and brown all get along, so almost every combination works. Toggle pieces on and off and watch the number.

    X

    Chapter ten

    Crimes against tailoring

    Twelve common mistakes. Tap each card for the fix.

    01

    The sleeve label

    That little brand tag stitched to the cuff.

    Cut it off

    It's there for the shop, not for you. Snip the threads carefully with small scissors the day you buy the jacket.

    02

    Stitched-shut vents

    An X of loose thread holding the back vent closed.

    Snip the basting

    That temporary stitch keeps the jacket neat in the shop. Remove it from the vents (and usually the pockets) before wearing.

    03

    Bulging pockets

    Phone, wallet, keys and a charger, all in the jacket.

    Carry less

    A slim cardholder, phone in an inside pocket, keys in a bag. Heavy pockets wreck the line of a jacket and stretch the cloth for good.

    04

    Shin flash

    Short socks and a stretch of hairy leg when you sit.

    Over-the-calf socks

    Or at least mid-calf. With suits and dress trousers, skin should never appear between your sock and your hem.

    05

    Mismatched leathers

    Black shoes with a tan belt.

    Match them

    Belt and shoes in the same colour family. Owning one black and one dark-brown belt solves this forever.

    06

    The visible undershirt

    A crew-neck vest peeking out of an open collar.

    Go V-neck or none

    If you wear an undershirt with an open collar, choose a deep V-neck in a colour close to your skin tone (not white), so it disappears.

    07

    Square-toed shoes

    The chunky, boxy dress shoes of the early 2000s.

    Go for a rounded last

    A gently rounded or slightly chiselled toe looks classic. Very square or very pointy both look dated.

    08

    The short-sleeve tie

    A tie with a short-sleeved shirt.

    Pick one

    Ties are for long sleeves. In the heat, go tieless with a short-sleeved shirt, or wear a light linen long-sleeved one with the sleeves rolled.

    09

    Accessory overload

    Tie bar, lapel pin, bracelet, chain, two rings, a giant watch.

    Edit

    Pick two or three. A watch, a wedding ring, perhaps a pocket square. Restraint looks confident; too much looks like you're trying.

    10

    The wrinkled shirt

    Creases on the chest, cuffs and collar.

    Iron or steam

    A hand steamer takes two minutes. Or hang shirts in the bathroom while you shower. Crisp cotton reads as "I have my life together".

    11

    Shiny suits

    A glossy sheen on a business suit.

    Choose matte wool

    Shine usually means synthetic fibres or over-pressing. Matte wool looks richer and more serious. Save shine for satin lapels at night.

    12

    Too much fragrance

    Smelled before you're seen.

    Two sprays, max

    Neck and chest. Fragrance should be discovered by people close enough to hug you, not announced to the whole lift.

    XI

    Chapter eleven

    The finishing touches

    Grooming, accessories and looking after your things.

    The best outfit in the world won't survive bad grooming. The good news is that grooming is mostly habit, and costs very little.

    ✂

    Hair

    Find a barber you like and go every 3 to 5 weeks. Ask what suits your face shape and hair type, and how to style it at home. A good cut grown out is better than a great one neglected.

    ◐

    Face

    Clean-shaven or bearded, both are fine. What matters is that it looks deliberate. Keep a beard trimmed, with a clean neckline about a finger's width above the Adam's apple. Moisturise. Use sunscreen.

    ✋

    Hands

    Short, clean nails. People notice hands when you shake, pass a menu, hold a glass. Thirty seconds with clippers once a week.

    ◷

    A watch

    One simple, good watch outdoes a drawer of flashy ones. A slim dress watch on leather, or a steel sports watch, covers almost everything. (We wrote a whole guide on this.)

    ✿

    Fragrance

    One or two you love, applied lightly. Fresh and citrusy for day and summer, warmer and woodier for evening and winter. Spray onto skin, not clothes.

    ❖

    Glasses

    The top of the frame should roughly follow your eyebrows. Contrast your face shape: round faces suit angular frames, square faces suit rounder ones.

    Care: making good things last

    A gentleman doesn't necessarily own more clothes. He looks after them better.

    Suits

    Rest & brush

    Hang jackets on wide, shaped wooden hangers. Give a suit a day off between wears. Brush it with a clothes brush instead of dry-cleaning, which wears out the fabric. A couple of times a year is plenty.

    Shirts

    Wash cool, hang dry

    Unbutton the collar and cuffs before washing. Remove collar stays. Hang to dry and iron while slightly damp. Skip the tumble dryer to make them last years longer.

    Knitwear

    Fold, never hang

    Hangers stretch the shoulders out of shape. Fold knits in a drawer, with cedar blocks to keep moths away. Hand-wash wool and lay it flat to dry.

    Shoes

    Polish monthly

    Clean, condition, polish. Cedar trees after every wear. Get them resoled when the sole thins; good leather shoes can be rebuilt many times.

    XII

    Chapter twelve

    Manners maketh man

    The part that matters more than everything above it.

    The phrase is about 650 years old. It's the motto of an English school founded in 1382, and it meant that your behaviour, not your birth, makes you who you are. That's the modern gentleman in one line. You can be perfectly dressed and still fail every test that counts.

    Etiquette sounds stuffy, but most of it is just a shared playbook for making people feel at ease. Here's the part that still matters.

    Be on time

    Being late tells people your time matters more than theirs. If you're going to be late, message before the start time, not after. For dinner at someone's home, arriving 5–10 minutes after the stated time is kinder than arriving early.

    Put the phone away

    Not face-down on the table: away. A phone on the table says "you have my attention until something better comes along." Give people the respect of all of it.

    Listen more than you talk

    Ask questions, and actually listen to the answers. Remember details and bring them up later. The people everyone calls charming are usually just good listeners.

    Handshakes & greetings

    Stand up to greet someone. Firm (not crushing), two or three pumps, eye contact, a smile. Follow the other person's lead: in many cultures a nod, a bow or a hand on the heart is the custom.

    Introduce people

    Never leave two people standing awkwardly next to you. Introduce them, and give each a hook: "Priya, this is Tom. Tom's just back from Lisbon."

    Treat staff well

    How you treat waiters, drivers, cleaners and receptionists is the truest measure of your manners. Please, thank you, eye contact, patience. Tip fairly.

    Say thank you, properly

    After a dinner, a favour or a gift, send a thank-you message within a day. A handwritten card is rarer now, and so it means even more.

    Good manners online

    Reply within a reasonable time. Don't leave people on "read" for something that needs an answer. Ask before posting photos of other people. Don't say on a screen what you wouldn't say to a face.

    At the table

    A formal table setting looks intimidating but follows a simple logic. Hover over (or tap) any piece to learn what it's for.

    A few more table rules, quickly

    • Wait until everyone is served (or the host starts) before eating.
    • Pass dishes to the right, and pass salt and pepper together, always, even if someone only asks for one.
    • Bring food to your mouth, not your mouth to the food. Elbows off the table while eating.
    • Small bites, mouth closed, no talking while chewing.
    • Excuse yourself briefly if you need to leave the table. No explanation needed.

    Test yourself

    Eight everyday situations. What would a gentleman do?

    Here's the strange thing about dressing well: done right, nobody notices your clothes. They notice you. You look sharp, at ease, like someone who has things in order. Then you hold a door, remember someone's name and ask a good question, and they decide you're the kind of person they'd like to know.

    That's all a modern gentleman is. Not a man with money or a title or a wardrobe full of tweed. Just someone who takes care, of how he shows up and of the people around him. The clothes are the easy part. You've just learned most of it.

    Notes & sources

    About the illustrations

    Every figure on this page is drawn in code (SVG) by a single paper-doll function, so each slider and button redraws him from scratch. He's deliberately faceless, like a fashion sketch, so you can picture yourself instead. The fit sliders exaggerate problems so they're easy to see; real fit issues are usually subtler. Formality scores in the dressing room are a rough guide based on the consensus of the sources below, not a law of nature.

    On "rules"

    Classic menswear rules come mostly from British and American tailoring traditions. They're a useful starting point, not a universal truth. Customs around dress, greetings and dining vary across cultures, and the most gentlemanly thing is always to respect the customs of the place you're in and the people you're with. Learn the rules, then break them on purpose.
