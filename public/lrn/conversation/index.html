Good Company

The art of conversation, for men. How to talk to friends, strangers and the woman across the room so that every one of them walks away glad they sat next to you.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 50 min · 10 interactives

Pull up a chair

In the 1870s, a young American in London is said to have dined, on consecutive evenings, beside the two great rivals of British politics. Asked afterwards what she made of them, Jennie Jerome, later the mother of Winston Churchill, reportedly said that after sitting next to William Gladstone she left thinking he was the cleverest man in England. After sitting next to Benjamin Disraeli, she left thinking she was the cleverest woman.

Nobody can quite pin down the original source of that story, and it has been retold so often it has gone smooth like a river stone. But it survives for a reason. It describes the two kinds of men you meet at every dinner, wedding, office party and bar. One talks to impress you. The other talks so that you impress yourself. You admire the first. You want to see the second again.

This is a guide to becoming the second kind of man.

It is not a book of lines. Lines are what you reach for when you have nothing to say, and the people worth talking to can smell them from across the room. What follows is closer to a craft: a set of habits, some old as Aristotle and some tested in psychology labs in the last decade, that turn talking into one of the great pleasures of your life rather than a performance you dread.

We'll go through the science of why conversations go better than you think, the inner game that makes charm possible, how to open, how to keep things going, how to listen (the part most men skip), how to go deeper, how to tell a story and land a joke, and how to talk to women without becoming a cliché. Along the way we'll borrow from the men, real and fictional, who made it look easy: Bond and Darcy, Cary Grant and Anthony Bourdain, Jim Halpert and a North Korean captain who barely says a word.

I

Chapter one

You're better at this than you think

Three studies that should change how nervous you feel before saying hello.

Most men who think they're bad at conversation are not actually bad at it. They're bad at predicting it. Before a conversation, the mind runs a disaster film: the awkward pause, the blank stare, the polite escape to the bathroom. Psychologists have spent the last fifteen years testing whether that film is accurate. It almost never is.

Before reading on, make a guess for each of these. Tap the answer you believe.

Chicago, 2014 · Epley & Schroeder

Commuters are asked to talk to a stranger on the train, sit alone, or do whatever they normally do. Who has the best ride?

The talkers, clearly. Across nine experiments with hundreds of Chicago-area commuters, people who chatted with a stranger reported a more pleasant journey than people who sat in solitude, even though beforehand they'd predicted the opposite. The commuters said they'd happily talk; they just assumed the person next to them wouldn't want to. That assumption was wrong. A later replication on London trains found the same thing.

Yale & Cornell, 2018 · Boothby et al.

Two strangers chat for five minutes. Afterwards, how much does each think the other liked them, compared to how much they really did?

They underestimate it, and by a lot. The researchers named it the liking gap. It showed up after five-minute chats, after forty-five-minute chats, among students and in the general public. The shyer the person, the bigger the gap. The cause seems to be the post-mortem: we replay our own clumsy moments in detail while the other person barely noticed them. Their conclusion is worth taping to your mirror: people like us more than we know.

Chicago & Texas, 2021 · Kardas, Kumar & Epley

Strangers are given either small-talk questions or deep ones (“What are you most grateful for?”). Which conversations go better than expected?

Both beat expectations, but the deep ones surprised people most. Across twelve experiments with more than 1,800 people, participants expected deep talk to be awkward and found it warmer, more connecting and no more uncomfortable than small talk. The main reason we stay shallow is that we underestimate how much the other person wants to go deeper too. When people were told about this bias, they chose deeper questions.

Put those together and a picture forms. The fear that stops you from talking to people is built on three bad forecasts: that they won't want to talk, that they won't like you, and that anything meaningful will be awkward. All three are systematically wrong, and wrong in your favour.

This matters more than any technique, because the single biggest predictor of whether you'll get good at conversation is how many conversations you have. Every guide, including this one, is useless to the man who stays home. The research gives you permission to stop waiting until you feel ready.

The awkward pause you're dreading lasts three seconds. The regret of not saying hello lasts all the way home.

II

Chapter two

The inner game

Charm isn't something you do. It's a state you're in, and other people catch it.

Watch the most effortlessly charming man you know and try to write down what he actually says. It's usually unremarkable. “Where did you grow up?” “No! Then what happened?” “That's brilliant.” If you said the same words in the same order you'd get a fraction of the effect. That's because charm lives less in the words than in the state behind them, and people read that state long before they parse a sentence.

Olivia Fox Cabane, who taught charisma at Stanford and wrote The Charisma Myth, breaks it into three ingredients. The useful thing about her model is that all three can be practised.

Presence

The feeling that you have someone's complete attention. Cabane calls it the foundation for the other two. People who met Bill Clinton, whatever they thought of his politics, describe the same thing: for the ninety seconds he spoke to you, you were the only person in the room. Presence is fragile. A glance at your phone, a look over their shoulder at who else has arrived, and it's gone, and they feel it.

Warmth

Goodwill toward the other person: the sense that you're on their side and glad they exist. It shows up in the eyes before the mouth. Warmth without power can read as eager to please; power without warmth reads as arrogant. A trick Cabane suggests for faking it until it's real: before you walk in, think of someone you love, or your dog, and carry that softness into the room.

Power

Not status or money: the impression that you're at ease and capable of handling whatever comes. It's mostly body language and pace. You take up your space, you don't rush, you don't fidget, you're comfortable with a pause. Power is what lets a man tease, disagree, or go quiet without anyone worrying. It's what makes his warmth feel like a gift instead of a request.

Curiosity is the engine

Dale Carnegie's most famous piece of advice, from 1936, is that you'll make far more friends by becoming genuinely interested in other people than by trying to get them interested in you. Ninety years later it's still the best single sentence ever written on the subject. The modern phrasing is be interested, not interesting.

The secret is that this is a perspective, not a tactic. Every person you meet knows things you don't, has lived through things you haven't, and has at least one subject on which they could talk for an hour with their eyes lit up. Your job in a conversation is to find it. Treat each person as a puzzle worth solving and the anxiety drains away, because your attention has moved off yourself and onto them. Self-consciousness and curiosity can't occupy the same head at the same time.

Anthony Bourdain built a career on this. He was a cook and a writer, not a trained journalist, yet strangers across the world opened up to him over plastic stools and cheap beer. He showed up hungry, asked about the food, the grandmother who made it, the street it came from, and then actually listened to the answer. He never pretended to be the expert at someone else's table.

Drop the need to impress

The tension most men feel in conversation comes from wanting something from it: approval, a date, to be thought clever. That wanting leaks out as trying too hard, talking too fast, laughing too loud, steering every topic back to their achievements. The fix is a quiet mental shift: I'm not here to be judged, I'm here to find out who this person is. You're the one evaluating whether this is an interesting conversation, too.

Call it abundance. A man who'll be fine whether or not this particular chat goes well is relaxed, and relaxation is magnetic. A man who needs it to go well is tense, and tension is contagious.

Reframe the nerves

You will still feel nervous sometimes. Good. The physical signals of anxiety (fast heart, alertness, energy) are nearly identical to those of excitement. Harvard's Alison Wood Brooks found that people who told themselves “I am excited” before a stressful performance did better than those who tried to calm down. You don't have to get rid of the adrenaline. Just give it a better name.

Small habits of presence

  • Phone away, face down, or better, in a pocket. Researchers have found that just having a phone visible on the table makes conversations feel less close.
  • Turn your feet and chest toward them, not just your head.
  • Let them finish. Then wait one more beat before you answer. That pause says you were listening, not loading.
  • Eye contact, softly. Hold it while they talk; it's natural to look away briefly while you think.
  • Slow down. Nervous men talk fast. Confident men talk at the speed of a man who expects to be listened to.

III

Chapter three

The first ten seconds

How to start a conversation without a line.

Here is a secret every socially easy person knows: it barely matters what you say first. The opener's only job is to signal “I'm friendly and I'd like to talk.” People respond to the warmth and the ease, not the wit. “Hi, I'm Arjun” works far more often than you'd believe. A clever opener delivered nervously loses to a boring one delivered with a smile.

That said, some openers make the next minute easier. The best come from the one thing you and the other person share: the moment you're both in. Art of Manliness calls it the ARE method, after Debra Fine's and Carol Fleming's work on small talk: Anchor on something in the shared situation, Reveal something about yourself, then Encourage them to talk with a question.

A

Anchor

“This queue is moving like it's on a tea break.”

R

Reveal

“I came for the cardamom bun everyone keeps posting about.”

E

Encourage

“Is it worth the wait, or have I been fooled by Instagram?”

Notice that the question at the end asks for an opinion, not a fact. Opinions are generous: they let the other person be the expert, they have no wrong answer, and they invite a second sentence. “Have you been here before?” gets a yes or a no. “What's good here?” gets a story.

Pick a setting below and shuffle for openers that work, with a note on why.

Small talk is not the enemy

Men who pride themselves on “hating small talk” often misunderstand what it's for. Small talk isn't the conversation; it's the handshake before the conversation. It lets two people check each other's tone, humour and safety before risking anything real. Skip it entirely and you seem intense. Get stuck in it forever and you seem dull. The art is to use it as a runway: a minute or two of weather, food and how-do-you-know-the-host, while you listen for the thread that leads somewhere better.

Which brings us to the most useful skill in this guide.

IV

Chapter four

Pulling threads

You will never run out of things to say again.

The fear of running out of things to say is the most common one men describe. It comes from treating conversation like a list of topics you have to supply. In fact, every sentence the other person says hands you several new topics for free. The skill is noticing them.

Coaches call it threading. Take any answer, find the interesting nouns and feelings hidden in it, and pick one to pull. Try it: tap a highlighted thread in the reply below.

One innocent “how's your week?” and you have five directions to go, each of which will hand you five more. Running dry is impossible once you start listening for threads. The bonus is that people love being asked about the specific thing they mentioned, because it proves you heard them.

The magic of the second question

In 2017, a team at Harvard led by Karen Huang recorded hundreds of conversations, including real speed dates, and counted the questions people asked. People who asked more questions were liked more, and speed-daters who asked more questions got more second dates. But one kind of question did nearly all the work: the follow-up question, one that builds on what the other person just said.

That makes sense. A switch question (“Cool. So do you have any pets?”) says you were waiting for your turn. A follow-up (“Wait, you moved there alone at nineteen? What was that first month like?”) says you were listening and want more. The researchers called it responsiveness, and it's the closest thing to a cheat code the psychology of liking has found.

Switch

“Nice. Where did you go to college?”

Changes the subject. Feels like a form.

Mirror

“Your sister's wedding?”

Repeat their last few words with a lift. Invites more.

Follow-up

“How did you end up giving the speech?”

Builds on what they said. The one that wins dates.

Feeling

“Were you nervous, or secretly loving it?”

Moves from facts to how it felt. Goes deeper.

Don't interrogate: the tennis rule

There's a trap here. A man who has just learned that questions make people like him will sometimes fire off twenty in a row, and the date starts to feel like a job interview, or a deposition. Conversation is a rally, not a serve machine. You ask, they answer, you give something back: a reaction, a related story, an opinion, and then pass it over again. The other person should feel they're learning about you too.

Watch the three styles below. Most bad conversations are one of the first two.

Give them handles

The flip side of threading is offering threads yourself. When someone asks what you do, “I'm an analyst” is a closed door. “I'm an analyst at a bank. Mostly I build spreadsheets that predict which loans will go bad, which makes me very popular at weddings” gives them four things to pull: banking, prediction, the joke, and the fact that you can laugh at yourself. This is free information, and generous people drop it constantly. It makes it easy for the other person to be a good conversationalist too.

If you ever do stall, the old FORD list (Family, Occupation, Recreation, Dreams) is a decent emergency kit. But treat it like a spare tyre: there to get you home, not something you drive on every day.

V

Chapter five

The lost art of listening

The best talkers are, almost without exception, the best listeners.

Art of Manliness puts it plainly: the key to the art of conversation isn't in the talking, it's in the listening. Most of us don't really listen. We wait. While the other person speaks, we're rehearsing our next line, and the moment there's a gap we fire it off.

The sociologist Charles Derber spent years recording dinner-table conversations and found a pattern he called conversational narcissism: the steady, mostly unconscious habit of turning the focus back to yourself. He noticed two kinds of response. A shift response moves attention to you. A support response keeps it on them.

Friend “I'm exhausted. I've been working until midnight all week.”

Shift

“Tell me about it. I had three deadlines on Monday alone.”

Support

“All week? What's going on, is it a big launch?”

The shift response isn't evil. You're trying to relate. But do it three times in a row and the other person quietly gives up, because every story they start ends up being about you. Good listeners use support responses until the other person has finished, then share their own experience as a gift, not a takeover. Test yourself on six real moments:

Borrowing from a hostage negotiator

Chris Voss spent decades as the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator, a job in which bad listening gets people killed. Two of his core tools translate surprisingly well to a dinner party.

Mirroring: repeat the last one to three words someone said, with a slight upward lift. “I've just quit my job.” “Quit your job?” It sounds too simple to work, and it works beautifully. It tells the other person you're tracking them and invites them to keep going without you steering. Use it sparingly or it sounds like a parrot.

Labelling: name the feeling you hear. “It sounds like you're actually relieved.” “Seems like that really got under your skin.” You're not agreeing or fixing; you're showing you understood the emotion underneath the facts. When you get it right, people visibly relax. When you get it slightly wrong, they correct you, and the conversation goes deeper anyway.

Turning toward

The psychologist John Gottman, who has studied couples for more than forty years, describes something he calls a bid: any small attempt to get another person's attention or connection. “Look at that bird.” “Ugh, my boss emailed again.” A sigh. Each bid can be turned toward (engaged with), turned away from (ignored), or turned against (dismissed).

In one study, newlyweds who were still married six years later had turned toward each other's bids about 86 percent of the time in the lab. Those who later divorced had done so about 33 percent of the time. It's the most romantic statistic in social science, and it isn't just about marriage. Every conversation is a stream of tiny bids. Friends, colleagues and the woman you just met are all quietly checking whether you notice them.

Remember the small thing

The most powerful listening act happens after the conversation is over. She mentioned her interview on Thursday; on Friday you text, “How did it go?” He said his father was in hospital; next time you see him, you ask first. Nothing makes people feel more valued, and almost nobody does it. If your memory is bad, write it down in your phone afterwards. Presidents and great salespeople have done this for a century. So can you.

Names

Carnegie claimed a person's name is, to them, the sweetest sound in any language. Say it back when you meet (“Meera, good to meet you”), use it once more during the conversation, and once when you leave. If you've forgotten it, admit it early and gracefully: “I'm so sorry, tell me your name again, I want to get it right.” Admitting it in the first five minutes is charming. Admitting it in the fifth week is not.

VI

Chapter six

Climbing the ladder

From weather to what keeps them up at night, one rung at a time.

Psychologists have known since the 1970s that closeness grows through self-disclosure that escalates gradually and reciprocally. I tell you something slightly personal; you match it; I go a little further. Arthur Aron turned this into an experiment in 1997, giving pairs of strangers 36 questions that got steadily more intimate. After 45 minutes, many felt closer to their partner than to people they'd known for years. (Two participants from an early version of the study reportedly went on to marry.)

You don't need a list of 36 questions. You need to understand the ladder. Conversations move through rough levels, and people feel comfortable when you climb at the pace they're climbing. Rush up three rungs at once and it's unnerving. Never leave the bottom rung and it's forgettable.

How to climb gracefully

Go first, a little. Asking “What's your biggest regret?” cold is an ambush. Saying “I almost studied architecture instead. I still wonder about it. Is there a road you nearly took?” offers something before you ask for something. People match the level of openness you show.

Ask why. The easiest way up the ladder is a single word. “I'm a dentist.” “Why dentistry?” Behind every fact is a reason, and behind every reason is a value.

Ask for stories, not summaries. “What was the best trip you've ever taken?” is fine. “What's a moment from a trip that you still think about?” gets you a scene, a feeling and a person.

Watch their face. If they lean in, keep climbing. If they give a short answer and change the subject, step down a rung, no harm done. Depth is something you offer, not something you extract.

Facts tell you what someone did. Opinions tell you how they think. Feelings tell you who they are.

VII

Chapter seven

Stories, wit and the light touch

When it's your turn to talk, make it worth listening to.

Being a good listener doesn't mean being a blank wall. At some point people want to know about you, and how you tell them matters. The men people describe as great company almost always have a handful of good stories and a light, easy sense of humour. Both can be learned.

The thirty-second story

Most men tell stories the way they'd write a police report: in order, with every detail, and the interesting part buried near the end. A good conversational story is short, has a hook up front, something at stake, a turn, and an ending that lands, then it hands the floor back. Tap the button to see a rambling story lose its fat.

Keep three or four of these in your back pocket: an embarrassing travel story, a time something went wrong at work, how you met your best friend, the strangest job you ever had. Tell each one a few times and you'll notice which details get the laugh and which ones you can drop. Comedians call that working out material. You can call it being prepared.

One rule: end with a hand-off. “...anyway, that's why I can't eat prawns. Have you ever had a holiday go that wrong?” A story that ends with a question turns a performance back into a conversation.

Humour: play, not performance

Women in survey after survey list a sense of humour near the top of what they find attractive in men. But what's usually meant is not “tells good jokes”. It's playfulness: the ability to notice what's funny about a situation, laugh at yourself, and invite the other person into the joke. A few principles:

  • Situational beats prepared. A comment about the wobbly table, the overly dramatic waiter or the song that just came on will always feel more alive than a joke you brought from home.
  • Self-deprecation, in moderation. Laughing at your own small failings shows confidence. Running yourself down constantly shows the opposite. Mock your parallel parking, not your worth.
  • Callbacks are gold. Referring back to something funny from earlier in the conversation creates an instant inside joke. It also proves you were listening.
  • Laugh generously. Being a good audience is half of being funny. People remember who found them funny.
  • Exaggerate for effect. “This is the best samosa in human history” is more fun than “this is good.” Commit to the bit.

Teasing, done right

Gentle teasing is one of the fastest ways to build rapport, between friends and in flirting, because it signals you're comfortable enough not to be formal. It goes wrong when it hits something the person is actually insecure about. Tease choices, never bodies or backgrounds: her alarming loyalty to a terrible football team, his eleven-step coffee order, her conviction that pineapple belongs on pizza. Keep it obviously affectionate, and if it doesn't land, drop it and move on. The aim is a shared smile, not a win.

VIII

Chapter eight

Talking to women

The same rules, plus a little more courage and a lot more care.

Here is the honest truth that most dating advice won't sell you: there is no special language for talking to women. Everything in this guide (presence, curiosity, threading, follow-up questions, support responses, stories, play) works on women because it works on people. The men who are effortless with women are usually just effortless with everyone. They talk to the waiter, the bouncer and the grandmother at the wedding with the same easy interest.

The difference is mostly in you. Attraction raises the stakes, the stakes trigger the need to impress, and the need to impress kills everything in Chapter Two. So the first and most important skill is to talk to the woman you find attractive exactly as you'd talk to anyone you found interesting. Curious first. Attracted second.

What tends to work

  • Attention over performance. Ask about her, remember what she says, follow up on the specific thing. Being truly seen is rare and very attractive.
  • Compliment choices, not body parts. Her book, her laugh, her taste in music, the way she described her city. Specific beats generic: “You tell a story like a novelist” lands; “You're hot” doesn't.
  • Be a little playful. Flirting is mostly sincere interest plus a bit of fun. Light teasing, a playful challenge (“Prove it”), a shared joke at the world's expense.
  • Have a point of view. Agreeing with everything is not charming. A man who'll cheerfully disagree about a film is more interesting than one who nods.
  • Be clear about your interest. At some point, say it. “I've really enjoyed talking to you. Can I take you for a drink sometime?” Clarity is confident and kind.

What tends to backfire

  • Lines and routines. They're about you performing, not about her.
  • Interrupting or explaining. Explaining her own field or experience to her is the fastest way to end the evening.
  • Talking about money, conquests or exes to impress. It doesn't.
  • Negging, the old pick-up trick of disguised insults. It confuses insecurity with confidence.
  • Ignoring signals. Short answers, turned shoulders, eyes on her phone or on her friends: she's done. Wrap up gracefully.
  • Persistence after a no. In films it's romantic. In life it's frightening.

Reading interest, and respecting it

You can't read minds, and you shouldn't try to “decode” body language with a checklist. But broadly, people who are enjoying a conversation give longer answers, ask you questions back, laugh easily, turn toward you, and look for ways to keep talking. People who aren't give short replies, stop asking questions and angle away. The single most attractive thing you can do when you see the second pattern is to leave warmly and without sulking: “I'll let you get back to your friends. Lovely to meet you.” Grace in a small rejection tells everyone watching what kind of man you are.

Remember too that approaching a woman out in the world is different from talking to someone at a party where everyone is there to mingle. A woman on her commute, at the gym, or with headphones on has told you something. Read the room before you read the menu.

A word about Bond

We'll meet James Bond properly in the next chapter, and there's a great deal to learn from him. But it's worth saying up front: take his composure, his wit and his attention to detail, and leave his 1960s manners in the 1960s. Some of the scenes in the early films that once played as seduction now play as harassment. The modern version of Bond's charm is all the cool with none of the coercion.

IX

Chapter nine

The charm files

Seventeen men, real and invented, and the one thing each does better than anyone.

Novelists, screenwriters and actors have spent centuries working out what makes a man magnetic in conversation, then testing it on millions of readers and viewers. The heroes women keep swooning over are, in effect, a giant experiment. Interestingly, they're rarely the best-looking men in their stories. What they share is a way of paying attention.

Flip each card for the lesson. Filter by fiction or real life.

What they have in common

Lay all these men side by side and a pattern appears. Hardly any of them are big talkers. Ri Jeong-hyuk barely speaks. Darcy is famously bad at small talk. Bond is economical to the point of terseness. Fred Rogers was comfortable with silences that would make most television producers faint. What they share is attention, ease and sincerity. They notice. They're unhurried. They mean what they say. And nearly every one of them makes the people they talk to feel more interesting, more capable or more understood than they felt before.

That is the Disraeli effect, in seventeen different accents. And the encouraging part is that the real men on this list mostly built it on purpose. Archie Leach, a poor boy from Bristol, invented a man called Cary Grant and then played him so long he became him. Grant reportedly said that he pretended to be the man he wanted to be until, eventually, he was that man. You're allowed to do the same.

X

Chapter ten

Friends, groups and the long game

Talking to people you already know is its own art.

With strangers the goal is to connect. With friends the goal is to keep connecting, which is harder than it sounds. Old friendships slide into ruts: the same jokes, the same complaints, the same three topics. Men in particular tend to bond side by side, over sport, games or work, and can go years without asking each other a real question. Many men who have plenty of mates have no one they can call at 2 a.m.

You can be the one who changes that, without making it weird.

  • Ask the better question. Instead of “How's work?”, try “What's the best part of your week been?” or “What are you looking forward to?” or, with close friends, “How are you actually doing?” Then ask it again when they say “fine”.
  • Follow up on last time. “Did you ever sort things out with your brother?” shows you carry them around with you between meetings. That's what friendship is.
  • Bring something new. A question you've been chewing on, a story from the week, a recommendation. New input keeps friendships from going stale.
  • Banter, but not only banter. Mockery is how many men say “I love you.” Keep it. Just make sure there's room, sometimes, for the sincere version too.
  • Celebrate properly. When a friend shares good news, respond with real enthusiasm and questions. The psychologist Shelly Gable found that how partners respond to good news (eager and engaged versus flat or self-focused) predicts relationship quality as much as how they handle bad news.

Holding a group

Group conversation has different physics. The loudest person sets the topic, people split into pairs, and the quiet ones drift to the edge. The most socially intelligent man in a group isn't the one with the best stories; he's the one who notices who hasn't spoken and pulls them in. “Priya, you lived in Berlin, didn't you? Is it really like that?” It's a small kindness that makes you everyone's favourite without ever taking the spotlight.

A few more group rules: don't start a side conversation while someone's telling a story; if you interrupt, hand the floor back (“Sorry, go on, you were saying about the flat?”); and when you join a circle, listen for a minute before you speak, so you're joining their conversation, not replacing it.

XI

Chapter eleven

The graceful exit

Leave while they still want more.

Psychologists have a name for how we remember experiences: the peak-end rule. Daniel Kahneman and colleagues found that we judge an experience largely by its most intense moment and by how it ended, not by its average. Conversations are no exception. Leave on a laugh or a warm moment and the whole exchange glows in memory. Let it peter out into awkward silence and that's what gets remembered.

A study by Adam Mastroianni and colleagues found that most conversations don't end when either person wants them to: often one person wanted to leave earlier, or wanted to keep going. So don't wait for the perfect moment. Make one. The formula is simple: signal, appreciate, reconnect.

1

Signal

“I should go and find my friend before she sends a search party, but...”

2

Appreciate

“...I've loved hearing about the restaurant. You've completely sold me on Lisbon.”

3

Reconnect

“Let me get your number, I want to hear how the opening goes.”

Use their name, mention something specific from the conversation, and if you want to see them again, say so plainly. Then actually follow up, within a day or two, with a reference to something you talked about. That follow-up is where most of the friendships, opportunities and second dates in life actually come from.

Mistakes, and a month of practice

Reading about swimming won't keep you afloat.

In Pride and Prejudice, Darcy defends his awkwardness at a party by saying he simply lacks the talent some people have for conversing easily with strangers. Elizabeth, at the piano, replies that her fingers don't move over the keys as masterfully as other women's either, but she has always assumed that was her own fault, for not taking the trouble to practise. It's the best advice on conversation in all of English literature, and it's two hundred years old.

Last call

If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember this. Conversation is not a test you pass or fail. It's a small gift two people give each other: a few minutes of attention in a world that's short of it. The man who gives that gift generously, who listens like it matters, asks the second question, notices the quiet one in the corner, tells a good story and then hands the floor back, will never be short of good company.

Put your phone in your pocket. Go and say hello to someone. They'll probably like you more than you think.
