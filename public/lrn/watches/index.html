The Machine
on Your Wrist

How a wristwatch works, where it came from, and how to fall happily down the rabbit hole. A field guide for the newly curious.

By Aditya Chaubey · November 2026 · 35 min · 12 interactives

Wind it up

Your phone knows the time to within a few thousandths of a second. It gets it from satellites and cell towers, corrects itself constantly, and never needs winding. So why would anyone strap a little mechanical engine to their wrist that loses a few seconds a day, needs a service every few years and can cost as much as a car?

Ask a watch person and you'll get a strange mix of answers. Because it's beautiful. Because it was my grandfather's. Because there are more than a hundred tiny parts in there, beating eight times a second, powered by nothing but a coiled spring — and it works. Because it's the only machine most of us ever wear.

This is a guide for people who have just started to notice watches. We'll open one up and see how it ticks, meet the quartz crystal that nearly killed the Swiss watch industry, tour the styles and "complications", walk through five hundred years of history, and finish with the brands worth knowing and how to pick your first one.

The watch above is running on your computer's clock. Flip it between mechanical sweep and quartz tick and watch the seconds hand. That one difference — a glide versus a jump — is where our story starts.

I

Chapter one

Anatomy of a watch

Learn the words first. Everything else gets easier.

Watch people have a word for every millimeter of a watch, and the forums assume you know them all. Here's the outside of a typical sports watch. Tap the dots, or the names, to learn what each part does.

Under the dial sits the part that actually matters: the movement, also called the calibre. Everything else is housing. Two watches can look completely different and share the same movement inside, and two identical-looking watches can hide movements that differ in price by a factor of fifty. Most of the hobby is learning to tell the difference.

Movements come in two big families. A mechanical movement runs entirely on a wound spring and a ticking regulator made of metal. A quartz movement runs on a battery and a vibrating crystal. We'll start with the mechanical one, because it's the more astonishing of the two.

II

Chapter two

A spring, slowly letting go

Where a mechanical watch gets its energy.

Every mechanical watch has a battery, it's just made of steel. Inside a small drum called the barrel sits the mainspring: a ribbon of special alloy, often tens of centimeters long, coiled into a space smaller than a coin. Turn the crown and you wind it tighter. Left alone, it tries to unwind, and that patient push drives everything else.

A fully wound mainspring typically keeps a watch running for 40 to 80 hours, a figure called the power reserve. Some modern movements stretch to five days or more; a few specialist watches run for weeks. Try it: give the crown a few turns, then watch the reserve drain. Time here runs very fast.

Switch to Automatic and move your pointer (or finger) back and forth across the panel. That's a self-winding watch: a half-moon weight called the rotor swings around the back of the movement every time your wrist moves, and a clever set of gears turns that wobble into winding. Wear it every day and it never needs touching. Take it off for the weekend, though, and it will probably have stopped by Monday.

Watch-speak: a hand-wound watch is called a "manual" or "manual-wind". An automatic is just "an auto". Both are mechanical. People who say "automatic" when they mean "not quartz" will get gently corrected online.

III

Chapter three

The gear train

From one turn every eight hours to eight beats a second.

A spring that pushes is useless on its own. If you let it unwind freely, it would empty itself in a fraction of a second. The trick is to make it unwind slowly and evenly, and the first part of that trick is a chain of gears called the going train.

The barrel holding the mainspring has teeth around its edge. It turns very slowly — in many movements, about once every seven or eight hours.

It drives the center wheel, which is geared to turn exactly once an hour. Its arbor pokes up through the middle of the dial and carries the minute hand. (A separate little gearbox under the dial, the motion works, divides that by 12 for the hour hand.)

Next comes the third wheel, a go-between that keeps stepping the speed up.

Then the fourth wheel, which turns once a minute. Put a hand on it and you have a seconds hand. That's why on many older watches the seconds sit in a little subdial near 6 o'clock: that's simply where the fourth wheel happened to be.

Last in line is the escape wheel, with oddly shaped teeth. Each wheel in the chain spins faster than the one before, and with less force: by the time the spring's push reaches here, it is tiny. Something has to stop this wheel from simply whizzing round.

That something is the escapement — a pallet fork and a balance wheel — and it's the real genius of the watch. It lets the escape wheel move forward one tooth at a time, in perfectly timed beats. Everything upstream moves in tiny, regular steps as a result. Let's zoom in.

IV

Chapter four

The heartbeat

A wheel that swings, a fork that rocks, and a sound you can hear.

The balance wheel is the watch's timekeeper. It's a small, carefully weighted ring attached to a hair-thin coiled spring, the hairspring. Twist it and let go, and it swings back and forth like a pendulum, at a rate that barely changes whether the swing is big or small. That steadiness is what makes timekeeping possible.

Between the balance and the escape wheel sits the pallet fork, a little lever with two jewels on its arms. Each time the balance swings through the middle, it knocks the fork from one side to the other. The fork unlocks the escape wheel, which jumps forward a fraction of a tooth — and as it does, it gives the fork, and through it the balance, a tiny push to keep it swinging. Then the other jewel catches the next tooth and locks the wheel again.

Lock, unlock, push, lock. That's the "tick-tock" — literally the sound of jewels hitting teeth. Below it is slowed down a lot.

Watchmakers measure this rhythm in vibrations per hour (vph), where every tick and every tock counts. A modern movement at 28,800 vph beats 8 times a second, so its seconds hand moves in 8 tiny steps per second. That's the smooth "sweep" people love, and it's not actually smooth at all: it's just stepping too fast for your eye to catch. Higher beat rates are a little more stable against bumps; lower ones wear more gently. Neither is "better" on its own.

Now think about the numbers. At 28,800 vph, a watch beats 691,200 times a day. To be accurate to five seconds a day, every single one of those beats has to be right to within a few millionths of a second. All of it powered by a spring, regulated by a hair, and rattled around on your wrist while you type, run and wash dishes.

A mechanical watch is about 200 years of very clever people fighting friction, gravity, temperature and magnetism — and mostly winning.

Why the jewels?

When a watch says "21 jewels" on the dial, it isn't bragging about bling. The jewels are tiny synthetic rubies used as bearings at the points that wear most: the pivots of the wheels and the pallet stones. Synthetic ruby is hard, smooth and holds oil well, which is why a good mechanical watch can run for decades. Around 17 to 25 jewels is normal for a time-only automatic. More jewels usually just means more functions, not more quality.

V

Chapter five

Then came quartz

A crystal that hums 32,768 times a second.

On Christmas Day 1969, Seiko began selling the Astron in Tokyo, the world's first quartz wristwatch. It was expensive and a little clunky. Within fifteen years, quartz had turned the entire watch industry upside down.

The idea is lovely. Quartz is piezoelectric: squeeze it and it makes a tiny voltage; apply a voltage and it flexes. Cut a sliver into the shape of a tiny tuning fork, put it in a circuit, and it vibrates at an extremely stable rate. In almost every quartz watch that rate is 32,768 times a second.

Why such an odd number? Because 32,768 is 2 multiplied by itself 15 times. A simple circuit can halve a frequency, and if you halve 32,768 fifteen times, you get exactly one pulse per second, which drives a tiny stepper motor that moves the seconds hand. Watch the chain below.

A quartz crystal is so steady that even a cheap quartz watch keeps time far better than the finest mechanical one. A typical quartz watch is rated to about ±15 seconds a month. A certified mechanical chronometer is allowed to drift −4 to +6 seconds a day. Race them and see what happens over a year.

Quartz was cheaper, more accurate and nearly maintenance-free. Through the 1970s and early 1980s, as Japanese and American companies flooded the market with quartz watches, the Swiss industry, which had bet on mechanical, collapsed. Historians call it the quartz crisis. Roughly two-thirds of Swiss watchmaking jobs disappeared and hundreds of brands vanished or were sold.

What saved it was, of all things, a cheap plastic quartz watch. In 1983 a Swiss group launched the Swatch: colorful, fun, made from just 51 parts, assembled by machines. It sold in the millions, and the profits helped rebuild the industry. Then something unexpected happened. Freed from having to be the most accurate way to tell time, mechanical watches reinvented themselves as something else entirely: craft objects, heirlooms, little bits of engineering art. By the 2000s they were more desirable than ever.

That's the paradox at the heart of the hobby. People pay more for the less accurate watch, because accuracy stopped being the point.

Chapter six

What's inside, and what it was built for.

The second way is by style. Almost every watch style started life as a tool for a specific job: diving, flying, racing, crossing time zones. Most people who buy them today never dive or fly a plane, but the design details stuck, and each one has a reason. Pick a style to see what makes it what it is.

VII

Chapter seven

Complications

Everything a watch does besides telling the time.

In watch-speak, a complication is any function beyond hours, minutes and seconds. A date window is a complication. So is a stopwatch. At the far end are watches with dozens of them, taking years to design and assemble. Each one below works: press buttons, pick cities, listen.

Watchmakers sometimes sort complications into tiers. Everyday ones like the date and the GMT hand. Sporting ones like the chronograph. And the so-called grand complications: the perpetual calendar, the minute repeater and the split-seconds chronograph. A watch that combines several grand complications is a grande complication, and only a handful of workshops in the world can make one.

Five hundred years in fifteen stops

From pocket clocks to smartwatches.

Portable clocks are older than you'd think, and wristwatches younger. For most of history, a serious man's watch lived in his pocket. Wristwatches were for women, until a war changed everyone's mind. Drag or scroll through the timeline.

    IX

    Chapter nine

    The names worth knowing

    Who they are, and what makes each one interesting.

    There are hundreds of watch brands, but a few dozen come up again and again. Many big names now belong to a handful of groups: Swatch Group (Omega, Longines, Tissot, Breguet and more), Richemont (Cartier, Jaeger-LeCoultre, Vacheron Constantin, IWC, A. Lange & Söhne) and LVMH (TAG Heuer, Hublot, Zenith, Bulgari). A few of the most famous are still independent or family-owned. Filter by price range to find your level.

      A word on "in-house": you'll see brands boast about "in-house movements", designed and made by the brand itself. Many excellent watches instead use movements bought from specialist makers such as ETA (owned by Swatch Group) or Sellita, or from Seiko and Miyota in Japan. Neither is a scam. In-house adds prestige and sometimes clever engineering. A proven outsourced movement is usually cheaper to buy and to service.

      X

      Chapter ten

      Your first (real) watch

      Size, budget, water and care.

      The best advice in the hobby is old and boring: buy what you love, start cheaper than you think, and wear it a lot before buying the next one. Here are the tools to get the details right.

      1. Will it fit?

      Case diameter gets all the attention, but the number that decides whether a watch fits is lug-to-lug: the distance from the tip of the top lugs to the tip of the bottom ones. If it's wider than the top of your wrist, the lugs hang over the edge and it looks too big. Measure your wrist with a strip of paper.

      3. Can it get wet?

      Water-resistance ratings are among the most misunderstood numbers in watches. They come from static pressure tests in a lab, not from real depths, and moving your arm through water creates spikes of extra pressure. A "30 m" watch isn't one to swim with.

      And always: screw the crown down if it's a screw-down crown, never press chronograph pushers underwater unless the watch says you can, and get the seals checked every few years.

      4. Looking after it

      01

      Service it

      A mechanical watch's oils dry out over time. Most brands suggest a full service every 5–10 years. It isn't cheap, so factor it in.

      02

      Mind the date

      On many watches, changing the date between about 9 pm and 3 am, while the date gears are engaged, can damage them. Move the hands past 6 first.

      03

      Beware magnets

      Strong magnets in speakers, laptop lids and bag clasps can magnetize a hairspring and make a watch suddenly run minutes fast. A watchmaker can demagnetize it in seconds.

      04

      Wind it gently

      Hand-wind until you feel resistance, then stop. Autos can be hand-wound too, and you can't overwind them: a slipping clutch protects the spring.

      5. A pocket glossary

      Here's the thing nobody tells you when you start: you will probably end up caring less about the watch than you expected, and more about what it represents. A watch you wore on the day you graduated. A cheap diver that's been on every trip. The one you inherited, that still runs if you give the crown a few turns.

      The mechanism is a marvel: a spring letting go, a wheel swinging, a fork rocking eight times a second, a few hundred years of ingenuity folded into a space the size of a coin. But the real complication, as watch people like to say, is the person wearing it.

      Go wind something.
